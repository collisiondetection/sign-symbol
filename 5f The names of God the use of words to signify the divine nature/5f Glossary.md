# Glossary — 5f. The names of God: the use of words to signify the divine nature

Key terms and concepts discussed in this section, each with a representative quote drawn directly from the citations below.

## Borrowed Divine Names

Herodotus's deflationary account of how the Greeks came to have names for their gods at all: imported wholesale from Egypt, then merely ratified — not originated — by the oracle at Dodona, which was asked only whether to adopt the foreign names, not what the gods' true names were. It treats divine naming as a matter of cultural transmission and institutional approval rather than revelation.

> "Not long after the arrival of the names they sent to consult the oracle at Dodona about them. This is the most ancient oracle in Greece, and at that time there was no other. To their question, 'Whether they should adopt the names that had been imported from the foreigners?' the oracle replied by recommending their use."
> — Herodotus, *History*, Bk II [60a-d]

## Divine Ineffability

Augustine's paradox: to call God "unspeakable" is already to have spoken of Him, so the very word meant to mark the limit of language quietly breaks it. He concludes that silence, not further speech, is the truer response to the contradiction — even as he goes on, of necessity, speaking anyway.

> "Have I spoken of God, or uttered His praise, in any worthy way? Nay, I feel that I have done nothing more than desire to speak; and if I have said anything, it is not what I desired to say... And so God is not even to be called 'unspeakable,' because to say even this is to speak of Him."
> — Augustine, *Christian Doctrine*, Bk I, Ch 6

## God as a Relative Name

Newton's careful distinction, in the General Scholium, between God's absolute attributes — eternal, infinite, perfect — and the name "God" itself, which he argues signifies something else entirely: lordship, a relation to servants. A being however perfect, he insists, is not thereby a "God" unless it also has dominion; the word names a relationship, not merely a nature.

> "...for God is a relative word, and has a respect to servants... The word God usually signifies Lord; but every lord is not a God. It is the dominion of a spiritual being which constitutes a God."
> — Newton, *Principles*, Bk III, General Scholium

## He Who Is

Aquinas's argument, drawn straight from Exodus, that "He Who Is" is the single most proper name available for God, because unlike every other name it signifies no determinate form or mode of being, only being itself — "an infinite and indeterminate sea of substance," as Damascene put it, which Aquinas approvingly cites.

> "This name, he who is, is most properly applied to God, for three reasons. First, because of its signification. For it does not signify form, but being itself."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 13, A 11

## I Am Who Am

The divine self-naming at the burning bush, where God answers Moses's request for a name not with a proper name among other names but with a declaration of pure existence itself. This passage anchors the whole section: nearly every later writer who asks what it means to name God returns to it, above all Aquinas, who argues centuries later that it is the most proper name precisely because it signifies being itself rather than any particular nature.

> "God said to Moses: I AM WHO AM. He said: Thus shalt thou say to the children of Israel: HE WHO IS, hath sent me to you."
> — Exodus, 3:13-14

## Naming as Honor, Not Comprehension

Hobbes's deflationary account of why we call God anything at all: not because any name captures His nature — He is by definition incomprehensible — but because refusing to name Him, or naming Him carelessly, would fail to show Him the honor due a sovereign beyond our understanding.

> "And therefore the name of God is used, not to make us conceive Him (for He is incomprehensible, and His greatness and power are unconceivable), but that we may honour Him."
> — Hobbes, *Leviathan*, Part I

## Reverent Agnosticism About the Gods' Names

Socrates' sudden check on his own etymologizing in the *Cratylus*: whatever ingenious derivations mortals propose, the gods' own names for themselves are unknown to us, and only their self-given names — not our guesses — can be called true. It is a rare moment of principled humility inside a dialogue otherwise devoted to confident, playful name-derivation.

> "Yes, indeed, Hermogenes; and there is one excellent principle which, as men of sense, we must acknowledge,—that of the Gods we know nothing, either of their natures or of the names which they give themselves; but we are sure that the names by which they call themselves, whatever they may be, are true."
> — Plato, *Cratylus*, 93d-97d

## The Analogy of Names

Aquinas's solution to how any word could apply to both God and creatures: neither univocally, in exactly the same sense (which would make God just another instance of a kind), nor equivocally, in a wholly unrelated sense (which would make theology impossible), but analogically — each name applying primarily to God and only secondarily, by derivation, to what He has caused.

> "Therefore it must be said that these names are said of God and creatures according to analogy, that is, according to proportion."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 13, A 5

## The First Namers of the Gods

Socrates' method in the *Cratylus*: treating the names of the gods as compressed philosophical claims, to be cracked open by etymology. His reading of "Zeus" as a fusion of "Zena" and "Dia" — one name, though divided — turns the god's very name into an argument, that he is the one "through whom all creatures always have life."

> "The name of Zeus... has also an excellent meaning, although hard to be understood, because really like a sentence, which is divided into two parts, for some call him Zena... and use the one half, and others who use the other half call him Dia... the two together signify the nature of the God... Wherefore we are right in calling him Zena and Dia, which are one name, although divided, meaning the God through whom all creatures always have life..."
> — Plato, *Cratylus*, 91c-d

## The Incommunicable Name

The Wisdom literature's diagnosis of the origin of idolatry: not primarily a philosophical error but a linguistic one, in which the unique name reserved for the true God is transferred, out of grief or flattery, onto a carved image. The passage treats the divine name itself as sacred property, capable of being misapplied to what cannot bear it.

> "And this was the occasion of deceiving human life: for men serving either their affection, or their kings, gave the incommunicable name to stones and wood."
> — Wisdom of Solomon, 14:20-21 (Apocrypha)

## The Name That Changes with the Tongue

In Dante's Paradise, Adam recalls that the name of God he used in Eden was not the name used afterward — "on earth called I; afterwards it was called El" — because human language itself is mortal and mutable, changing "as a leaf on a branch, which goes away and another comes." Even the name of the unchanging God turns out to be a casualty of changing human speech.

> "...the Supreme Good, whence comes the gladness that swathes me was on earth called I; afterwards it was called El; and that must needs me, for the custom of mortals is as a leaf on a branch, which goes away and another comes."
> — Dante, *Divine Comedy*, Paradise, XXVI [124-138]

## The Nameless God

Aeschylus's chorus addresses Zeus by a name it immediately confesses may not fit him at all, uncertain "whether he love that name or love it not." Voiced in the register of tragedy rather than theology, it is the same anxiety that will occupy Plato and Aquinas centuries later: that any single word offered to the divine might simply miss.

> "Zeus—whosoe'er He be, Whose state excels / All language syllables, / Knowing not so much / As whether He love that name or love it not;"
> — Aeschylus, *Agamemnon* [160-183]
