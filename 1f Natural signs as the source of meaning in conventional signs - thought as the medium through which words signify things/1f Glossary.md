# Glossary — 1f. Natural signs as the source of meaning in conventional signs: thought as the medium through which words signify things

Key terms and concepts discussed in this section, each with a representative quote drawn directly from the citations below.

## Words as Symbols of Mental Experience

Aristotle's founding statement of the whole section's thesis: spoken words are conventional and vary by language, yet what they symbolize — the mental experience itself — does not vary. A word is therefore a sign at one remove: it signifies a thought, and the thought in turn is a natural likeness of the thing, which is why people who share no language whatsoever still share the same underlying mental life.

> "Spoken words are the symbols of mental experience and written words are the symbols of spoken words. Just as all men have not the same writing, so all men have not the same speech sounds, but the mental experiences, which these directly symbolize, are the same for all, as also are those things of which our experiences are the images."
> — Aristotle, *Interpretation*, CH 1

## The Natural-Fitness Theory of Names

Socrates's opening position in the *Cratylus*: before there was voice at all, people wanting to communicate would have imitated things bodily — raising the hands to mean lightness, dropping them to mean heaviness — and vocal naming is simply this same imitative instinct transferred to sounds, so that particular letters (rho for motion, lambda for smoothness) are held to naturally resemble the qualities they name.

> "We should imitate the nature of the thing; the elevation of our hands to heaven would mean lightness and upwardness; heaviness and downwardness would be expressed by letting them drop to the ground."
> — Plato, *Cratylus*

## Convention Completes Resemblance

By the end of the same dialogue, Socrates walks the theory back: the letter lambda, supposedly expressive of softness, turns up in a word for hardness anyway, and the two of them can still understand each other perfectly well. What actually carries the meaning across, he concludes, is not likeness of sound to thing but shared custom — resemblance may have started language, but agreement is what sustains it.

> "The signification of words is given by custom and not by likeness, for custom may indicate by the unlike as well as by the like... Custom and convention must be supposed to contribute to the indication of our thoughts."
> — Plato, *Cratylus*

## The Word of the Heart

Aquinas's account of an inner, unspoken word that precedes and grounds every spoken one: the very act of understanding something produces, within the mind, a conception of the thing understood — and it is this silent mental conception, not the sound itself, that the audible word merely goes on to express. Aquinas uses this psychological structure as the model for how the divine Word (the Son) proceeds from the Father's understanding.

> "Whenever we understand, by the very fact of understanding there proceeds something within us, which is a conception of the object understood, a conception issuing from our intellectual power and proceeding from our knowledge of that object. This conception is signified by the spoken word; and it is called the word of the heart signified by the word of the voice."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 27, A 1

## The Language Test for Thought

Descartes's proposed criterion for distinguishing a genuine mind from a mere mechanism, animal included: not the ability to produce words — a parrot manages that — but the ability to arrange words appropriately in response to whatever is said, on any subject whatever. Only a thinking being behind the words can improvise that way; everything else is disposition of organs, however lifelike the output sounds.

> "It is evident that magpies and parrots are able to utter words just like ourselves, and yet they cannot speak as we do, that is, so as to give evidence that they think of what they say."
> — Descartes, *Discourse on Method*, Part V

## Ideas as the Medium Between Words and Things

Locke's empiricist version of the section's thesis: a word does not attach to a thing directly, but to an abstract idea in the mind, and that idea sits between the word and the thing it is ultimately about. Both the accuracy of our knowledge and the intelligibility of our speech depend on this middle term doing its job — on our ideas actually corresponding, on one side to the things, and on the other to the customary meaning of the name.

> "This abstract idea, being something in the mind, between the thing that exists, and the name that is given to it; it is in our ideas that both the rightness of our knowledge, and the propriety and intelligibleness of our speaking, consists."
> — Locke, *Human Understanding*, BK II, CH XXXII

## Language as the Source of Abstract Ideas

Berkeley's reversal of the picture Locke and the scholastics share: philosophers don't first form abstract general ideas and then invent words to label them; rather, the practical need for economical naming — one word standing in for indefinitely many particular things — is itself what creates the illusion that a single, precise abstract idea must exist to justify the single name.

> "If there had been no such things as speech or universal signs there never had been any thought of abstraction."
> — Berkeley, *Human Knowledge*, Intro, Sect 18

## The Primacy of Words Among Signs

Augustine's survey of every channel by which people signal their thoughts to one another — nods, gestures, military flags, the sound of a trumpet — concludes that words hold a unique and irreplaceable rank among them. Every other kind of sign can be translated into words and explained that way, but the reverse is never possible: no combination of gestures or flags could ever capture what a sentence says.

> "For among men words have obtained far and away the chief place as a means of indicating the thoughts of the mind... I have been able to put into words all those signs, the various classes of which I have briefly touched upon, but I could by no effort express words in terms of those signs."
> — Augustine, *Christian Doctrine*, BK II, CH 3
