# Glossary — 5a. Natural things as signs of divinity

Key terms and concepts discussed in this section, each with a representative quote drawn directly from the citations below. This is a sample glossary for one section — see the note at the end.

## Analogical Predication

The scholastic doctrine that words applied to God (*good*, *wise*, *living*) are neither used in exactly the same sense as when applied to creatures (univocally) nor in a wholly unrelated sense (equivocally), but *analogically* — creatures partially and imperfectly represent a perfection that exists in God without limit. This is the theoretical basis for how any created thing can function as a "sign" of God at all: it resembles its cause enough to point toward it, without being identical to it.

> "Therefore these names signify the divine substance, but in an imperfect manner, even as creatures represent it imperfectly."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 13, A 2

## Argument from Design

The inference from the observed order, regularity, and apparent purposefulness of the natural world to the existence of an intelligent governing cause — later called the teleological argument. Aquinas grounds it in ordinary experience: order implies an orderer, the way a tidy house implies someone tidied it.

> "If we enter a well ordered house we gather from it the intention of him who put it in order."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 103, A 1 (citing Aristotle via Cicero)

## Book of Nature

The idea, running through Bacon and much of this section, that creation itself is a second scripture alongside the written Word — one authored not in words but in things, to be read through observation rather than exegesis.

> "Let no man... think or maintain, that a man can search too far, or be too well studied in the book of God's word, or in the book of God's works, divinity or philosophy."
> — Bacon, *Advancement of Learning*

## Celestial Harmony

Kepler's conviction that the mathematical ratios governing planetary motion are not merely descriptive but doxological — that astronomy, done rightly, is a form of worship, and the order of the heavens is itself a hymn to their Maker.

> "I commence a sacred discourse, a most true hymn to God the Founder... I am free to give myself up to the sacred madness."
> — Kepler, *Harmonies of the World*

## Miracle

Distinguished in this section from ordinary providence: not simply anything wondrous, but a deliberate suspension of nature's own laws by their Author, for a specific and legible end — never performed, in Bacon's telling, merely to prove a point that reason could already establish on its own.

> "Thou never workest miracles, but to a divine and excellent end... the laws of nature are Thine own laws, and Thou exceedest them not but upon great cause."
> — Bacon, *New Atlantis*

## Natural Theology

Bacon's term for the branch of knowledge concerning God derived not from revelation but from reasoning about the created world — deliberately bounded: sufficient to refute atheism, but not sufficient to constitute faith.

> "Divine philosophy or natural theology... is that knowledge or rudiment of knowledge concerning God, which may be obtained by the contemplation of his creatures... The bounds of this knowledge are, that it sufficeth to convince atheism, but not to inform religion."
> — Bacon, *Advancement of Learning*

## Nature as Divine Language

Berkeley's immaterialist twist on the whole section's theme: sensible objects aren't signs pointing to some separate, hidden God behind them — they *are* God's speech, directly and continuously addressed to us, so that seeing anything at all is already an encounter with divinity.

> "We do at all times and in all places perceive manifest tokens of the Divinity: everything we see, hear, feel, or anywise perceive by sense, being a sign or effect of the power of God."
> — Berkeley, *Human Knowledge*

## Natural Variety as Testimony

The argument, here voiced in comic rather than theological register, that the sheer abundance and diversity of created things is itself evidence of divine wisdom and power — showing the idea reaching even into the novel, not just scripture and scholastic argument.

> "A variety which, as it must give great pleasure to a contemplative beholder, so doth it admirably display the power, and wisdom, and goodness of the Creator."
> — Fielding, *Tom Jones*

## Plenitude

Aquinas's answer to why creation is diverse rather than uniform: no single creature could adequately represent God's infinite goodness, so multiplicity itself — the sheer variety of things — is necessary to reflect a perfection no one thing could hold alone.

> "His goodness could not be adequately represented by one creature alone, He produced many and diverse creatures, that what was wanting to one in the representation of the divine goodness might be supplied by another."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 47, A 1

## Providence in Small Things

The Stoic argument that divine care is most convincingly visible not in grand cosmic events but in the fitness of the smallest, most easily overlooked details of the natural order.

> "Is there anything less useful than the hair on the chin? What then, has not nature used this hair also in the most suitable manner possible?"
> — Epictetus, *Discourses*, Bk I, Ch 16

## Sign (Signum)

The master concept of this whole Syntopicon chapter: something whose very existence or structure points beyond itself to something else. In this section specifically, a natural thing functions as a sign of the divine when its order or beauty is legible as evidence of an intelligent, invisible cause.

> "For the invisible things of him, from the creation of the world, are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made; his eternal power also, and divinity."
> — Romans 1:20

## Vestiges of the Trinity

Augustine's search for reflections of the divine Trinity not only in scripture but structurally, within the human mind itself — arguing that the triad of being, knowing, and loving in every person is a natural "footprint" of the Three Persons.

> "We are, and know that we are, and delight in our being, and our knowledge of it... as we run over all the works which He has established, we may detect, as it were, His footprints."
> — Augustine, *City of God*, Bk XI, Ch 26/28

## Wonder as Broken Knowledge

Bacon's careful distinction between two different outcomes of studying nature: contemplating creatures *as creatures* yields real, cumulative knowledge; contemplating them as a route *to God Himself* yields only wonder — genuine, but necessarily partial and unfinished.

> "The contemplation of God's creatures and works produceth (having regard to the works and creatures themselves) knowledge, but having regard to God, no perfect knowledge, but wonder which is broken knowledge."
> — Bacon, *Advancement of Learning*
