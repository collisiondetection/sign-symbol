# Glossary — 3c(3). The same word used to signify an attribute and its cause or effect

Key terms and concepts discussed in this section, each with a representative quote drawn directly from the citations below.

## "Good" as Intrinsic Quality vs. Productive Result

Aristotle's own methodological instruction for handling a word with several senses: it is not enough to note that "good" applies both to virtues like justice and to things like healthy food — one must explain *why* the senses differ. Justice and courage are called good because of a quality intrinsic to them; food and medicine are called good only because they produce a further, separate result — the same word naming an attribute in one case and naming whatever causes that attribute in the other.

> "We must not merely say that justice and courage are called ‘good’ in one sense, and that what conduces to vigour and what conduces to health are called so in another, but also that the former are so called because of a certain intrinsic quality they themselves have, the latter because they are productive of a certain result and not because of any intrinsic quality in themselves."
> — Aristotle, *Topics*, Bk I, Ch 15, [106a1-9] 149d

## Focal Meaning

Aristotle's broader theory covering the whole pattern: a term like "being," or his standing example "healthy," is not used in one identical sense of everything it names, nor merely ambiguously either — every use instead relates back to one central, defining case. Food is "healthy" because it preserves health, medicine because it produces health, a complexion because it is a symptom of health: different relations, but all pointing to a single focus.

> "There are many senses in which a thing may be said to ‘be’, but all that ‘is’ is related to one central point, one definite kind of thing, and is not said to ‘be’ by a mere ambiguity. Everything which is healthy is related to health, one thing in the sense that it preserves health, another in the sense that it produces it, another in the sense that it is a symptom of health, another because it is capable of it."
> — Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, Bk IV, Ch 2, [1003a33-b11] 522b

## "Healthy" Applies Primarily to the Animal

Aquinas draws out the order of priority hidden inside this pattern: when several things share a name because of their different relations to one primary instance, that primary instance belongs in the very definition of all the others. "Healthy" said of medicine means "the cause of health in the animal"; "healthy" said of urine means "the sign of the animal's health" — so both secondary uses can only be defined by first mentioning the animal's own health, never the reverse.

> "Healthy applied to animals comes into the definition of healthy applied to medicine, which is called healthy as being the cause of health in the animal, and also into the definition of healthy which is applied to urine, which is called healthy in so far as it is the sign of the animal's health."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 13, A 6

## Denomination from a Common Term

Aquinas applies the identical causal pattern to an entirely different case: the word "sacrament." Just as an animal, medicine, diet, and urine can all be called "healthy" through four different relations to one health — possessing it, causing it, preserving it, or signifying it — several different things can likewise be called "sacrament" through different relations to one sanctity, though in the strict theological sense only the sign-relation among these counts as a sacrament proper.

> "All things that are ordered to one, even in different ways, can be denominated from it; thus, from health which is in an animal, not only is the animal said to be healthy through being the subject of health, but medicine also is said to be healthy through producing health, diet through preserving it, and urine, through being a sign of health."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part III, Q 60, A 1

## One Truth Reflected in Many

Aquinas extends the same cause-and-sign pattern to truth itself. In one sense there are as many truths as there are created intellects that grasp something correctly; in another sense there is only a single Truth, since every created truth is caused by, and exists only as a reflection of, the one divine intellect from which the others take their name — much as urine is called "healthy" only by reflecting the animal's own health.

> "As from one man’s face many likenesses are reflected in a mirror, so many truths are reflected from the one divine truth."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 16, A 6
