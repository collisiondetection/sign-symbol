# Glossary — 1e. The invention of non-verbal symbols: money, titles, seals, ceremonies, courtesies

Key terms and concepts discussed in this section, each with a representative quote drawn directly from the citations below.

## Bodily Imitation

In a digression within the *Cratylus* on how a voiceless humanity might still communicate, Socrates argues that gesture is not arbitrary but naturally mimetic — the very shape of a sign (hands raised for lightness, hands dropped for heaviness) resembles the thing it signifies, so that the body, like the voice, can only ever express something by imitating it.

> "Suppose that we had no voice or tongue, and wanted to communicate with one another, should we not, like the deaf and dumb, make signs with the hands and head and the rest of the body?... We should imitate the nature of the thing; the elevation of our hands to heaven would mean lightness and upwardness; heaviness and downwardness would be expressed by letting them drop to the ground..."
> — Plato, *Cratylus*, 104d-105a

## Civil Honour

Hobbes first catalogs an exhaustive natural grammar of honoring gestures — obeying, gift-giving, deference, praise — then adds a second, artificial tier peculiar to commonwealths: a sovereign can simply designate anything at all as a sign of honor by fiat, so that a title or office becomes honorable purely because the sovereign's will made it so.

> "In Commonwealths where he or they that have the supreme authority can make whatsoever they please to stand for signs of honour, there be other honours. A sovereign doth honour a subject with whatsoever title, or office, or employment, or action that he himself will have taken for a sign of his will to honour him."
> — Hobbes, *Leviathan*, Part I

## Honor as a Sign of Excellence

Arguing that happiness cannot consist in honor, Aquinas defines along the way what honor actually is: not a possession or a reward in itself, but a sign — an outward attestation that other people have recognized some genuine excellence already present in the person honored, rather than a thing that confers the excellence.

> "Honor is given to a man on account of some excellence in him; and consequently it is a sign and attestation of the excellence that is in the person honored."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I-II, Q 2, A 2

## Human Institutions

Sorting out which everyday conventions a Christian may use without superstition, Augustine names an entire class of ordinary non-verbal symbols — clothing that marks sex or rank, and above all the stamping and weighing of coins — as "human institutions": signs that hold force only because people within a given nation have tacitly agreed to honor them, which is exactly why the same signs differ from nation to nation.

> "Among the convenient and necessary arrangements of men with men are to be reckoned whatever differences they choose to make in bodily dress and ornament for the purpose of distinguishing sex or rank... and the arrangements as to weights and measures, and the stamping and weighing of coins, which are peculiar to each state and people... Now these, if they were not devices of men, would not be different in different nations..."
> — Augustine, *On Christian Doctrine*, Bk II, Ch 25

## Idol Ceremony

Alone before Agincourt, Henry V strips away every royal trapping in turn — crown, scepter, sword, robe, title — and finds nothing beneath them but "ceremony": a self-admittedly empty form that manufactures awe and obedience in other men while giving the king wearing it no more ease than any private soldier enjoys.

> "And what have kings that privates have not too, Save ceremony, save general ceremony? And what art thou, thou idol ceremony?... Art thou aught else but place, degree, and form, Creating awe and fear in other men..."
> — Shakespeare, *Henry V*, Act IV, Sc I

## Money as Convention

Working out how goods as unlike as a house and a pair of shoes can be fairly exchanged, Aristotle identifies money as the invented common measure that makes them commensurable at all — and roots its very Greek name in the fact that it is a creature of law and agreement rather than of nature, which is also why a community retains the power to devalue or abolish it.

> "Money has become by convention a sort of representative of demand; and this is why it has the name 'money' (νóμισμα) — because it exists not by nature but by law (νóμος) and it is in our power to change it and make it useless."
> — Aristotle, *Ethics*, Bk V, Ch 5

## Money by the Consent of Men

Locke locates the invention of money at the historical hinge between a state of nature bounded by what a person could actually use and a world of unequal accumulation: gold and silver do almost nothing to sustain life, so their entire value — and the disproportionate possession that follows from it — exists only because people came to a tacit agreement to treat them as precious.

> "Gold, silver, and diamonds are things that fancy or agreement hath put the value on, more than real use and the necessary support of life."
> — Locke, *Civil Government*, Ch V, Sect 46

## Outward Shows

Choosing among Portia's caskets, Bassanio reasons his way past gold and silver by distrusting exactly the kind of symbol this section catalogs: ornament, title, and outward mark routinely dress up a rotten interior — legal pleas, religious error, and simple cowardice all borrow some outward badge of virtue that has nothing to do with what actually lies beneath it.

> "So may the outward shows be least themselves. The world is still deceived with ornament... There is no vice so simple but assumes Some mark of virtue on his outward parts... Thus ornament is but the guilèd shore To a most dangerous sea..."
> — Shakespeare, *Merchant of Venice*, Act III, Sc II
