# Irony Glossary

Every genuine use of irony across the chapter's 32 sections — not just the word appearing, but irony as a real device someone is naming, deploying, or being accused of. Six entries, drawn from a handful of sources that between them account for all 15 occurrences of "irony"/"ironic"/"ironical" in the chapter's raw text.

## The Named Figure

Augustine's own definition, offered mid-catalogue of the figures of speech that "mean the very opposite of what they say." He distinguishes irony specifically by its delivery mechanism: it isn't the words that carry the reversal, but the tone of voice — the same sentence said straight would mean something else entirely.

> "In irony we indicate by the tone of voice the meaning we desire to convey; as when we say to a man who is behaving badly, 'You are doing well.'"
> — Augustine, *Christian Doctrine*, Bk III

*From [4c (1) The nature and utility of semantic analysis](4c%20%281%29%20The%20nature%20and%20utility%20of%20semantic%20analysis/4c%20%281%29%20The%20nature%20and%20utility%20of%20semantic%20analysis.md). The identical passage is also cited in 3a, 3c(1), and 5e.*

## Ironical and Mysterious

Hermogenes's complaint about Socrates, mid-dialogue in the *Cratylus* — pressed on whether names are correct by nature or by convention, Socrates won't give a straight answer, and Hermogenes names exactly what he's doing: hinting he has a real opinion while declining to simply state it. This is Socratic irony caught in the act, inside the very dialogue this whole project keeps returning to.

> "When I am anxious to have a further explanation he is ironical and mysterious, and seems to imply that he has a notion of his own about the matter, if he would only tell."
> — Plato, *Cratylus*

*From [2c Intrinsic and extrinsic denominations](2c%20Intrinsic%20and%20extrinsic%20denominations-%20the%20naming%20of%20things%20according%20to%20their%20natures%20or%20by%20reference%20to%20their%20relations/2c%20Intrinsic%20and%20extrinsic%20denominations-%20the%20naming%20of%20things%20according%20to%20their%20natures%20or%20by%20reference%20to%20their%20relations.md). The same passage is also cited in 1a.*

## A Patient's Ironical Humour

Freud reading a detail inside a patient's own dream — a joke about a car's speed — as the patient's real, waking sense of humor surviving intact into sleep. Irony here isn't imposed by the interpreter; it's evidence the dreamer's own personality is still recognizably present in the dream-work.

> "In the reference to the speed of the car the patient's ironical humour is given free play."
> — Freud, *Interpretation of Dreams*

*From [6a The symbolism of dreams their latent and manifest content](6a%20The%20symbolism%20of%20dreams%20their%20latent%20and%20manifest%20content/6a.%20The%20symbolism%20of%20dreams%20their%20latent%20and%20manifest%20content.md).*

## The Dream That Speaks Ironically

A sharper case: not the dreamer being witty, but the dream-thoughts themselves turning on the dreamer. Freud casts himself as forced to play the fool in his own dream, and reads the whole absurd scene as the unconscious mocking a rival by sarcastic reversal — saying the opposite of what it means, exactly as Augustine's definition requires.

> "The dream thoughts say ironically: 'Naturally, he is the fool, the lunatic, and you are the clever people who know better.'"
> — Freud, *Interpretation of Dreams*

## Is He Ironical, Is He Jesting?

Alyosha's interruption, mid-story, as Ivan narrates the Grand Inquisitor's address to the silent, returned Christ. The Inquisitor's speech is so contemptuous in substance and so respectful in form that Alyosha can't tell which register he's actually hearing — an ambiguity the text refuses to resolve for him.

> "'Is he ironical, is he jesting?' 'Not a bit of it! He claims it as a merit for himself and his Church...'"
> — Dostoevsky, *The Brothers Karamazov*

*From [5b Supernatural signs, omens, portents, visitations, dreams, miracles](5b%20Supernatural%20signs%2C%20omens%2C%20portents%2C%20visitations%2C%20dreams%2C%20miracles/5b%20Supernatural%20signs%2C%20omens%2C%20portents%2C%20visitations%2C%20dreams%2C%20miracles.md). The same scene is also cited in 5e.*

## The Calculated Smile

Prince Hippolyte tells a bad joke at a social gathering; before the room decides how to take it, Boris positions his own smile so that it can be read either way — genuine appreciation or gentle mockery — and lets the room's reaction retroactively decide which one it was. A deliberately unreadable sign, held that way on purpose.

> "Boris smiled circumspectly, so that it might be taken as ironical or appreciative according to the way the joke was received."
> — Tolstoy, *War and Peace*
