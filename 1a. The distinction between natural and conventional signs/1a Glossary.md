# Glossary — 1a. The distinction between natural and conventional signs

Key terms and concepts discussed in this section, each with a representative quote drawn directly from the citations below. This section opens the whole Syntopicon chapter and states its founding distinction, so its citations are unusually numerous and varied — the entries below range wider than most sections in this glossary set to reflect that.

## Naming as Original Institution

Genesis frames human naming as a single, immediate act with no visible process of negotiation: God parades the animals before Adam expressly "to see what he would call them," and whatever Adam calls each creature simply becomes its name on the spot — a scene later readers have read in opposite directions, some taking it to show names are naturally fitted to things by their maker, others taking it as proof that naming was, from the very first, an exercise of free human decision.

> "So out of the ground the LORD God formed every animal of the field and every bird of the air, and brought them to the man to see what he would call them; and whatever the man called every living creature, that was its name."
> — Genesis 2:19

## Psammetichus's Language Experiment

Herodotus reports an Egyptian king's attempt to discover humanity's original, most natural language empirically: two infants were raised by a shepherd under strict orders that no one ever speak a word in their presence, on the theory that whatever word the children eventually uttered on their own would unmask the one truly natural human tongue beneath all the world's conventional ones. (Their word, reportedly, turned out to be Phrygian for "bread" — so the Egyptians conceded the Phrygians, not themselves, were the older people.)

> "His object herein was to know, after the indistinct babblings of infancy were over, what word they would first articulate... the children both ran up to him with outstretched arms, and distinctly said "Becos.""
> — Herodotus, *History*, Bk II, 49a-c

## The Correctness of Names

The founding philosophical statement of this whole question, staged by Plato as a dispute Socrates is asked to arbitrate. Hermogenes argues that names are purely conventional, attached to things only by whatever agreement a community happens to adopt — a name is exactly as correct as any other a community might choose to substitute for it — while Cratylus, across the rest of the dialogue, presses the opposing claim that there is a single natural correctness to names, discoverable rather than merely decided, and the same for every nation.

> "I have often talked over this matter, both with Cratylus and others, and cannot convince myself that there is any principle of correctness in names other than convention and agreement; any name which you give, in my opinion, is the right one... for there is no name given to anything by nature; all is convention and habit of the users..."
> — Plato, *Cratylus*, 85a-114a,c (Hermogenes)

## Natural vs. Conventional Numerals

A precise, non-linguistic illustration of the same distinction. Nicomachus notes that the Greek practice of using letters as numerals — iota for 10, kappa for 20, omega for 800 — designates a quantity purely by agreement, requiring the code to be learned first; laying out one mark per unit counted (one stroke, then two, then three in a row) is, by contrast, a "natural, unartificial" sign of number, legible to anyone without any prior convention at all.

> "Each letter by which we indicate a number... designates that number by man's convention and agreement, not by nature. On the other hand, the natural, unartificial, and therefore simplest indication of numbers would be the setting forth one beside the other of the units contained in each."
> — Nicomachus, *Arithmetic*, Bk II, 832a

## Natural and Conventional Signs

The master definition underlying the whole topic, and the very terminology this section's title borrows directly. A sign, for Augustine, is anything that, beyond the impression it makes on the senses, brings something else to mind. Natural signs do this without any intention of signifying at all — smoke betraying fire, a footprint betraying a passing animal; conventional signs, by contrast, are deliberately exchanged between minds for the very purpose of making an inner thought or feeling known to someone else.

> "Now some signs are natural, others conventional. Natural signs are those which, apart from any intention or desire of using them as signs, do yet lead to the knowledge of something else, as, for example, smoke when it indicates fire... Conventional signs, on the other hand, are those which living beings mutually exchange for the purpose of showing, as well as they can, the feelings of their minds, or their perceptions, or their thoughts."
> — Augustine, *Christian Doctrine*, Bk II, Ch 1-2

## Notes of Cogitation

Bacon's own two-way division of all sign systems, arrived at independently of Augustine's: some "notes of cogitations" work through "some similitude or congruity" with the idea they express — hieroglyphics, gestures, a king silently topping his tallest garden flowers to signal a policy of cutting down the nobility — while others carry meaning purely *ad placitum*, by contract or acceptance alone, the way ordinary spoken and written words do.

> "These notes of cogitations are of two sorts; the one when the note hath some similitude or congruity with the notion: the other ad placitum, having force only by contract or acceptation. Of the former sort are hieroglyphics and gestures."
> — Bacon, *Advancement of Learning*, 62c-63a

## The Cry of Nature and the Invention of Conventional Signs

Rousseau's conjectural history traces an actual developmental sequence rather than a static taxonomy: the very first human "language" was not a sign system at all but an involuntary natural reflex — the instinctive cry provoked by danger or pain. Only later, as life grew more complex, did humans deliberately invent gestures and finally arbitrary vocal sounds, a substitution Rousseau explicitly calls the adoption of "conventional signs" — a change that, unlike the original cry, required a prior common agreement simply to function.

> "The articulate sounds of the voice, which, without bearing the same relation to any particular ideas, are better calculated to express them all, as conventional signs. Such an institution could only be made by common consent..."
> — Rousseau, *Inequality*, 341a-b
