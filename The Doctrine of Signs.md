# The Doctrine of Signs

*One footnote-length passage, in 1690, named the entire field this chapter's other companion reading, "The Conditioned Sign," already works inside — without either piece knowing it yet.*

Locke needed a word for the study of signs themselves. He built one from Greek, set it beside physics and ethics as one of exactly three branches of everything knowable — and then, within the same book, showed that the signs his new science studies are not all conditioned in the same way.

## Why signs need their own science

Ideas never leave the mind that has them. That, for Locke, is the whole problem signs exist to solve — and the reason he thinks studying them deserves to be its own science, not a footnote to logic or grammar.

> "Since the things the mind contemplates are none of them... present to the understanding, it is necessary that something else, as a sign or representation of the thing it considers, should be present to it: and these are ideas. And because the scene of ideas that makes one man's thoughts cannot be laid open to the immediate view of another, nor laid up anywhere but in the memory... therefore, to communicate our thoughts to one another... signs of our ideas are also necessary."
> — Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, Bk IV, Ch XXI, §4

Two layers of sign, not one. A thing in the world becomes an idea in a mind — already a kind of sign, already a step removed. That idea, being permanently locked inside its owner, then needs a second sign — a word, a sound — just to cross the gap into someone else's mind at all. Nothing else could do this job. You cannot hand someone your idea directly; you can only hand them a sign of it and trust the sign to provoke something like it in them.

## A third branch of everything

Locke doesn't propose a subfield. He proposes that all human knowledge divides into exactly three branches — and gives the study of signs equal standing with the other two, not a place underneath them: **Φυσική** (natural philosophy), **Πρακτική** (ethics), and **Σημειωτική** (the doctrine of signs).

He glosses his coinage plainly — and immediately hands it a second name, an older one, in case the new word doesn't stick:

> "The third branch may be called Σημειωτική, or the doctrine of signs; the most usual whereof being words, it is aptly enough termed also Λογική, logic: the business whereof is to consider the nature of signs the mind makes use of for the understanding of things, or conveying its knowledge to others."
> — Locke, *Human Understanding*, Bk IV, Ch XXI, §4

The transliteration of that first word — *sēmeiōtikē* — is the actual root of the modern term *semiotics*, picked up and formalized two centuries later by Charles Sanders Peirce and, independently, given the sibling term "sémiologie" by Ferdinand de Saussure. Worth being exact about what that claim covers and what it doesn't: within this Syntopicon chapter's 32 sections, this is the *only* passage where studying signs gets named as a discipline in its own right — neither Peirce nor Saussure appears anywhere else in the corpus. The line from this footnote to the modern field is real, well-documented history; it just isn't a story this particular chapter goes on to tell.

## Not every conditioned name is conditioned the same way

Having named the science, Locke immediately starts practicing it — and what he finds complicates the flat picture that "words are arbitrary" usually implies. He runs a thought experiment: Adam, newly made, needing words for things that have no names yet. Twice.

**A mixed mode — *kinneah* and *niouph*.** Adam notices Lamech's suspicion toward his wife, and coins two words — one for the suspicion itself, one for the disloyalty it fears — by freely combining ideas that exist only in his own head. There is no fact of the matter these words have to answer to. Whatever Adam meant by them is, automatically, exactly what they mean.

**A substance — *zahab*.** Adam's child finds a heavy yellow metal in the mountains and brings it home. Naming *this* is a different act entirely — the qualities going into the idea have to be taken from the thing itself, whether Adam notices all of them or not. The name can now simply be wrong about what it's naming.

Both words are equally arbitrary as **sounds** — "zahab" has no more natural claim on gold than "kinneah" has on suspicion. But as **ideas**, one answers to nothing outside the mind that made it, and the other answers to nature whether it likes it or not. Locke states the difference himself, directly, comparing the two acts side by side:

> "[In naming kinneah] he put ideas together only by his own imagination, not taken from the existence of anything... the standard there was of his own making. But in forming his idea of this new substance [zahab], he takes the quite contrary course; here he has a standard made by nature; and therefore... he puts in no simple idea into his complex one, but what he has the perception of from the thing itself."
> — Locke, *Human Understanding*, Bk III, Ch VI, §46

He returns to the same point from another angle entirely — not a thought experiment this time, but an observation anyone who has learned a second language can check for themselves: moral and social words routinely have no match at all in another tongue, because they were never tracking anything the far side of the world was obligated to also carve up the same way.

> "A moderate skill in different languages will easily satisfy one of the truth of this, it being so obvious to observe great store of words in one language which have not any that answer them in another... This could not have happened if these species were the steady workmanship of nature, and not collections made and abstracted by the mind, in order to naming, and for the convenience of communication."
> — Locke, *Human Understanding*, Bk III, Ch V, §8

"Gold" translates everywhere, more or less exactly, because zahab-type names are tracking something the whole world shares. Words like the Roman *versura* or the Jewish *corban* don't survive translation at all — not because some languages are careless, but because a kinneah-type name was never answerable to anything universal in the first place. The untranslatable word isn't a defect in the dictionary. It's the mind's own private standard, showing through.

## The science finds what the namer already knew

There's a real symmetry worth naming between this passage and the founding case in "The Conditioned Sign." Socrates, mid-dialogue in the *Cratylus*, watches his own theory that sound resembles sense fail on a counterexample he supplies himself, and concludes that custom does the real work — not because he set out to prove it, but because the evidence forced it. Locke, a philosopher rather than a dramatist, reaches a structurally similar refusal to oversimplify: not "all signs are arbitrary," full stop, but a genuine distinction between names that answer to nothing but their own maker and names that answer to the world whether the maker likes it or not.

What makes this passage worth a page of its own, rather than a paragraph inside the first one, is the gesture underneath the argument: Locke doesn't just theorize about conditioned signs — he coins one, in the same breath, to talk about the theorizing itself. *Sēmeiōtikē* is exactly the kind of word his own *kinneah* example describes: a sound he combined by his own choice, answerable to no prior archetype, adequate the moment he meant it. Three hundred years of a field carrying that name is the untranslatable-word argument, run in reverse — a private coinage that turned out to answer to something the whole world needed a word for after all.
