# Glossary — 1c. The things of nature functioning symbolically: the book of nature

Key terms and concepts discussed in this section, each with a representative quote drawn directly from the citations below.

## Things That Are Also Signs

Augustine's foundational distinction in *Christian Doctrine*: a "thing" and a "sign" are not mutually exclusive categories. Some objects are things and nothing more, but others — a particular piece of wood, a particular stone, a particular animal — remain ordinary things in themselves while also, on a given occasion, pointing beyond themselves to something further. This double status is the organizing principle of the whole section: nature's contents don't stop being what they are in order to mean something else.

> "Not, however, the wood which we read Moses cast into the bitter waters to make them sweet... nor the ram which Abraham offered up instead of his son; for these, though they are things, are also signs of other things."
> — Augustine, *Christian Doctrine*, BK I, CH 2

## Sign as Evidentiary Inference

Aristotle's logical account of what a sign is in argument: not a mystical correspondence but a demonstrative or generally-approved premise that licenses an inference from one thing's presence to another's — his example is inferring pregnancy from lactation. This grounds the section's more fanciful material (omens, portents, physiognomy) in a sober theory of how a natural fact can count as evidence at all.

> "A sign means a demonstrative proposition necessary or generally approved: for anything such that when it is another thing is, or when it has come into being the other has come into being before or after, is a sign of the other's being or having come into being."
> — Aristotle, *Prior Analytics*, BK II, CH 27

## Cosmic Symbolism

Kepler's Christianized Pythagoreanism: the physical structure of the universe is deliberately laid out to mirror doctrinal and psychological realities, down to precise geometric detail — the three regions of a sphere correspond to the three Persons of the Trinity, and the sun's relation to the planets corresponds to the heart's relation to the rest of the body. For Kepler, nature is symbolic not loosely or poetically but by exact design.

> "...there are three regions, symbols of the three persons of the Holy Trinity - the centre, a symbol of the Father; the surface, of the Son; and the intermediate space, of the Holy Ghost."
> — Kepler, *Epitome*, BK IV

## Portents at Birth and Death

The Shakespearean convention, dramatized across several history plays, that extraordinary natural events — comets, earthquakes, withering trees, bloody-looking moons — cluster around the birth or death of the great, as though nature itself takes editorial notice of political rupture. The device treats the natural world as a kind of chronicler, marking history's turning points with portents legible to anyone watching.

> "The bay-trees in our country are all wither'd, And meteors fright the fixed stars of heaven; The pale-faced moon looks bloody on the earth... These signs forerun the death or fall of kings."
> — Shakespeare, *Richard II*, Act II, Sc IV

## Skepticism Toward Astrological Signs

Edmund's soliloquy supplies the section's sharpest counter-argument: reading personal misfortune off the stars is a self-serving fiction, a way of relocating the blame for one's own faults onto "the sun, the moon, and the stars." Shakespeare places this rebuttal in the mouth of his most calculating villain, which only sharpens it as critique of the very portent-reading dramatized elsewhere in the history plays.

> "This is the excellent foppery of the world, that, when we are sick in fortune — often the surfeits of our own behaviour — we make guilty of our disasters the sun, the moon, and the stars... An admirable evasion of whoremaster man, to lay his goatish disposition to the charge of a star!"
> — Shakespeare, *King Lear*, Act I, Sc II

## Signs, Not Causes

Berkeley's idealist reworking of natural philosophy: what we take to be causal connections in nature — fire causing pain, motion causing sound — are really nothing more than a fixed correspondence, a language of "marks or signs" instituted by God for our instruction. On this view, the task of natural science is to read the vocabulary of that language correctly, not to uncover hidden mechanical causes behind it.

> "The connexion of ideas does not imply the relation of cause and effect, but only of a mark or sign with the thing signified. The fire which I see is not the cause of the pain I suffer upon my approaching it, but the mark that forewarns me of it."
> — Berkeley, *Human Knowledge*, Sect 65

## Mantic Interpretation

Hegel's account of how the Greeks specifically related to nature: unlike symbolic systems where meaning is felt to belong to the object itself, the interpreting seer (μάντις) actively supplies the significance of a natural occurrence — rustling oak leaves, the noise of subterranean water. Meaning does not simply radiate from nature; it is projected onto it by the questioning, comprehending human spirit.

> "The interpretation and explanation of nature and its transformations, the indication of their sense and import, is the act of the subjective spirit; and to this the Greeks attached the name μαντεία."
> — Hegel, *Philosophy of History*, Part II

## Nature as Pasteboard Mask

Ahab's philosophy, and arguably the most extreme statement of the section's whole theme: every visible object in nature is a thin shell concealing some "unknown but still reasoning thing" behind it, and the proper response to a natural sign is not calm reading but violent penetration — to strike through the appearance in search of whatever really moves it.

> "All visible objects, man, are but as pasteboard masks. But in each event — in the living act, the undoubted deed — there, some unknown but still reasoning thing puts forth the mouldings of its features from behind the unreasoning mask. If man will strike, strike through the mask!"
> — Melville, *Moby Dick*, Ch 36
