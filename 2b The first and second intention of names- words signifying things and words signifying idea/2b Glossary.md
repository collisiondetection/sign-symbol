# Glossary — 2b. The first and second intention of names: words signifying things and words signifying idea

Key terms and concepts discussed in this section, each with a representative quote drawn directly from the citations below.

## First and Second Intention

Aquinas's foundational distinction, borrowed from logic: a "first intention" name like *man* or *animal* signifies a real nature directly. A "second intention" name like *genus* or *species* signifies something else entirely — not a nature, but the logical status the mind confers on that nature once it has grasped it as common to many individuals. The two kinds of name look grammatically alike but point in completely different directions.

> "The names of genera and species, as man or animal, are given to signify the common natures themselves, but not the intentions of those common natures, signified by the terms genus or species."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 29, A 4

## Universality as an Added Intention

Aquinas locates exactly where second intention comes from: a nature such as humanity exists only in this or that individual man, never as such in reality — but when the intellect grasps that nature apart from any individual's particular conditions, the resulting object of thought acquires a further property, universality, that belongs to it only as thought, not as a thing in the world.

> "Humanity which is understood is only in this or that man, but that humanity is apprehended without the individual conditions, that is, that it be abstracted and consequently considered as universal, happens to humanity according as it is perceived by the intellect."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 85, A 2

## Names of Names

Hobbes's own three-tier taxonomy of words, which maps neatly onto first and second intention without using the scholastic vocabulary: some words name things, some name our private mental images of things, and a third class — "universal," "plural," "singular," "definition," "syllogism" — names not things or images at all, but *other words* and forms of speech.

> "Of which words, some are the names of the things conceived... others are the names of the imaginations themselves... and others again are names of names, or of different sorts of speech; as universal, plural, singular, are the names of names."
> — Hobbes, *Leviathan*, Part IV

## The Chimera Eating Second Intentions

Rabelais's send-up of scholastic excess: a mock book-title poses the "most subtle question" of whether a chimera humming in a vacuum could eat second intentions — a joke that only lands because the doctrine was real and, to Renaissance humanists, ripe for mockery as empty hairsplitting divorced from anything in the world.

> "Quæstio Subtilissima, utrum Chimæra in vacuo bombinans possit comedere secundas intentiones" [On the most subtle question: whether a Chimæra, humming in the Void, is able to eat Second Intentions]... those sly intentions of the mind, which in the school are called second notions.
> — Rabelais, *Gargantua and Pantagruel*, BK II / BK III

## Words Signify Ideas, Not Things Directly

Locke's empiricist recasting of the whole distinction: a word's immediate, proper signification is never the thing itself but the speaker's own idea of it — however incomplete that idea may be. A child who has only noticed a shining yellow color calls a peacock's tail "gold," because the word tracks his idea, not the metal's actual properties.

> "Words, in their primary or immediate signification, stand for nothing but the ideas in the mind of him that uses them, how imperfectly soever or carelessly those ideas are collected from the things which they are supposed to represent."
> — Locke, *Human Understanding*, BK III, CH II

## Universals as Creatures of the Understanding

Locke's nominalist verdict on second intention itself: generality is not a feature discoverable in things, since every existing thing is strictly particular. "General" and "universal" name something the mind does — a capacity it confers on a sign to stand for many particulars at once — not anything the sign, or the things it stands for, possess on their own.

> "General and universal belong not to the real existence of things; but are the inventions and creatures of the understanding, made by it for its own use... their general nature being nothing but the capacity they are put into, by the understanding, of signifying or representing many particulars."
> — Locke, *Human Understanding*, BK III, CH III

## Genus and Species Names Arise from Generalization

Lavoisier, borrowing from Condillac, grounds the abstract logical distinction in ordinary child development: a child first applies "tree" to one particular tree (first intention), then to the next similar thing, and by the time he's applied it many times over, the same word has quietly become the name of a class — an abstract idea comprehending all trees (second intention) — with no separate act of definition required.

> "A child... is taught to give the name tree to the first one which is pointed out to him. The next one he sees presents the same idea, and he gives it the same name... till at last the word tree, which he first applied to an individual, comes to be employed by him as the name of a class or a genus, an abstract idea, which comprehends all trees in general."
> — Lavoisier, *Elements of Chemistry*, Preface

## The Fallacy of Naming Thought by Its Object

James's psychological diagnosis of a mistake this whole distinction is meant to prevent: because ordinary language has no separate vocabulary for the *act* of thinking as opposed to the *object* thought about, people quietly assume the thinking must have whatever properties its object has — so an abstract object is assumed to require an equally abstract, freestanding "idea," when in fact the thought and its object belong to entirely different orders.

> "Naming our thought by its own objects, we almost all of us assume that as the objects are, so the thought must be. The thought of several distinct things can only consist of several distinct bits of thought, or 'ideas'; that of an abstract or universal object can only be an abstract or universal idea."
> — James, *Psychology*
