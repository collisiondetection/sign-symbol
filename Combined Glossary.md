## 1a. The distinction between natural and conventional signs

*[Full section text](1a.%20The%20distinction%20between%20natural%20and%20conventional%20signs/1a.%20The%20distinction%20between%20natural%20and%20conventional%20signs.md)*

### Naming as Original Institution

Genesis frames human naming as a single, immediate act with no visible process of negotiation: God parades the animals before Adam expressly "to see what he would call them," and whatever Adam calls each creature simply becomes its name on the spot — a scene later readers have read in opposite directions, some taking it to show names are naturally fitted to things by their maker, others taking it as proof that naming was, from the very first, an exercise of free human decision.

> "So out of the ground the LORD God formed every animal of the field and every bird of the air, and brought them to the man to see what he would call them; and whatever the man called every living creature, that was its name."
> — Genesis 2:19

### Psammetichus's Language Experiment

Herodotus reports an Egyptian king's attempt to discover humanity's original, most natural language empirically: two infants were raised by a shepherd under strict orders that no one ever speak a word in their presence, on the theory that whatever word the children eventually uttered on their own would unmask the one truly natural human tongue beneath all the world's conventional ones. (Their word, reportedly, turned out to be Phrygian for "bread" — so the Egyptians conceded the Phrygians, not themselves, were the older people.)

> "His object herein was to know, after the indistinct babblings of infancy were over, what word they would first articulate... the children both ran up to him with outstretched arms, and distinctly said "Becos.""
> — Herodotus, *History*, Bk II, 49a-c

### The Correctness of Names

The founding philosophical statement of this whole question, staged by Plato as a dispute Socrates is asked to arbitrate. Hermogenes argues that names are purely conventional, attached to things only by whatever agreement a community happens to adopt — a name is exactly as correct as any other a community might choose to substitute for it — while Cratylus, across the rest of the dialogue, presses the opposing claim that there is a single natural correctness to names, discoverable rather than merely decided, and the same for every nation.

> "I have often talked over this matter, both with Cratylus and others, and cannot convince myself that there is any principle of correctness in names other than convention and agreement; any name which you give, in my opinion, is the right one... for there is no name given to anything by nature; all is convention and habit of the users..."
> — Plato, *Cratylus*, 85a-114a,c (Hermogenes)

### Natural vs. Conventional Numerals

A precise, non-linguistic illustration of the same distinction. Nicomachus notes that the Greek practice of using letters as numerals — iota for 10, kappa for 20, omega for 800 — designates a quantity purely by agreement, requiring the code to be learned first; laying out one mark per unit counted (one stroke, then two, then three in a row) is, by contrast, a "natural, unartificial" sign of number, legible to anyone without any prior convention at all.

> "Each letter by which we indicate a number... designates that number by man's convention and agreement, not by nature. On the other hand, the natural, unartificial, and therefore simplest indication of numbers would be the setting forth one beside the other of the units contained in each."
> — Nicomachus, *Arithmetic*, Bk II, 832a

### Natural and Conventional Signs

The master definition underlying the whole topic, and the very terminology this section's title borrows directly. A sign, for Augustine, is anything that, beyond the impression it makes on the senses, brings something else to mind. Natural signs do this without any intention of signifying at all — smoke betraying fire, a footprint betraying a passing animal; conventional signs, by contrast, are deliberately exchanged between minds for the very purpose of making an inner thought or feeling known to someone else.

> "Now some signs are natural, others conventional. Natural signs are those which, apart from any intention or desire of using them as signs, do yet lead to the knowledge of something else, as, for example, smoke when it indicates fire... Conventional signs, on the other hand, are those which living beings mutually exchange for the purpose of showing, as well as they can, the feelings of their minds, or their perceptions, or their thoughts."
> — Augustine, *Christian Doctrine*, Bk II, Ch 1-2

### Notes of Cogitation

Bacon's own two-way division of all sign systems, arrived at independently of Augustine's: some "notes of cogitations" work through "some similitude or congruity" with the idea they express — hieroglyphics, gestures, a king silently topping his tallest garden flowers to signal a policy of cutting down the nobility — while others carry meaning purely *ad placitum*, by contract or acceptance alone, the way ordinary spoken and written words do.

> "These notes of cogitations are of two sorts; the one when the note hath some similitude or congruity with the notion: the other ad placitum, having force only by contract or acceptation. Of the former sort are hieroglyphics and gestures."
> — Bacon, *Advancement of Learning*, 62c-63a

### The Cry of Nature and the Invention of Conventional Signs

Rousseau's conjectural history traces an actual developmental sequence rather than a static taxonomy: the very first human "language" was not a sign system at all but an involuntary natural reflex — the instinctive cry provoked by danger or pain. Only later, as life grew more complex, did humans deliberately invent gestures and finally arbitrary vocal sounds, a substitution Rousseau explicitly calls the adoption of "conventional signs" — a change that, unlike the original cry, required a prior common agreement simply to function.

> "The articulate sounds of the voice, which, without bearing the same relation to any particular ideas, are better calculated to express them all, as conventional signs. Such an institution could only be made by common consent..."
> — Rousseau, *Inequality*, 341a-b

## 1b. The intentions of the mind: ideas and images as natural signs

*[Full section text](1b%20The%20intentions%20of%20the%20mind%20ideas%20and%20images%20as%20natural%20signs/1b%20The%20intentions%20of%20the%20mind%20ideas%20and%20images%20as%20natural%20signs.md)*

### An Echo from Elsewhere

Plotinus extends the Aristotelian picture of speech signifying thought into a full Neoplatonic hierarchy: uttered words are only the outermost link in a chain of signification, where spoken thought images the soul's own thought, and the soul's thought is itself already an echo of something higher still that it merely interprets.

> "As speech is the echo of the thought in the Soul, so thought in the Soul is an echo from elsewhere: that is to say, as the uttered thought is an image of the soul-thought, so the soul-thought images a thought above itself and is the interpreter of the higher sphere."
> — Plotinus, *First Ennead*, Tr II, Ch 3

### Conception, Not Perception

Defining the idea at the very start of the *Ethics*, Spinoza makes a deliberate terminological choice that reframes the whole natural-sign question: an idea is not something the mind merely receives, like a picture stamped onto a passive surface, but something the mind actively forms simply by virtue of being a thing that thinks.

> "By idea, I understand a conception of the mind which the mind forms because it is a thinking thing... I use the word conception rather than perception because the name perception seems to indicate that the mind is passive in its relation to the object. But the word conception seems to express the action of the mind."
> — Spinoza, *Ethics*, Part II, Def 3

### Constant Effects, Not Resemblances

Locke breaks the natural sign away from any requirement of resemblance: an idea like whiteness or pain answers for nothing actually present "in" the object the way a picture resembles its subject, yet it is still a real and true sign of that object, because what makes a sign trustworthy is simply its steady, reliable correspondence with the power that produces it, not any likeness to it.

> "Though whiteness and coldness are no more in snow than pain is; yet those ideas of whiteness and coldness, pain, &c., being in us the effects of powers in things without us... they are real ideas in us, whereby we distinguish the qualities that are really in things themselves... our ideas do as well serve us to that purpose, and are as real distinguishing characters, whether they be only constant effects, or else exact resemblances of something in the things themselves..."
> — Locke, *Human Understanding*, Bk II, Ch XXX, Sect 2

### Marks, Not Causes

Berkeley denies that one idea is ever truly the efficient cause of another — fire does not cause the pain of touching it — and replaces causation with signification: the whole observed order of nature is a language of marks and signs, instituted for our information by the same governing Spirit whose grammar the natural philosopher's real task is to decipher.

> "The connexion of ideas does not imply the relation of cause and effect, but only of a mark or sign with the thing signified. The fire which I see is not the cause of the pain I suffer upon my approaching it, but the mark that forewarns me of it... it is the searching after and endeavouring to understand those signs instituted by the Author of Nature, that ought to be the employment of the natural philosopher..."
> — Berkeley, *Human Knowledge*, Sect 65-66

### Objective Reality of an Idea

Descartes gives the classical resemblance-theory of ideas a technical, causal foundation: an idea signifies its object by containing that object's reality "objectively" — as represented content within the idea — and this representational content is real enough that it demands a cause adequate to explain it, which is precisely how Descartes turns the mere having of an idea into an argument for what exists outside the mind.

> "By the objective reality of an idea I mean that in respect of which the thing represented in the idea is an entity, in so far as that exists in the idea... For whatever we perceive as being as it were in the objects of our ideas, exists in the ideas themselves objectively."
> — Descartes, *Objections and Replies*, Def III-IV

### Speech Without Bodily Signs

Asking whether one angel can speak to another, Aquinas strips communication down to its bare essence: ordinary exterior speech needs some sensible sign, a voice or a gesture, but "to speak" really just means to make one's mental concept known to another, so a pure intellect can do it by will alone, with no bodily sign of any kind standing between one mind and the next.

> "Now when the mind turns itself to the actual consideration of any habitual knowledge, then a person speaks to himself; for the concept of the mind is called "the interior word." And by the fact that the concept of the angelic mind is ordered to be made known to another by the will of the angel himself, the concept of one angel is made known to another; and in this way one angel speaks to another; for to speak to another only means to make known the mental concept to another."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 107, A 1

### The Same for All

Opening the treatise on interpretation, Aristotle grounds the entire distinction between natural and conventional signs in a single observation: spoken words differ from language to language and writing differs from script to script, but the mental experience these signs point to, and the things that experience is an image of, are identical for every human being.

> "Spoken words are the symbols of mental experience and written words are the symbols of spoken words. Just as all men have not the same writing, so all men have not the same speech sounds, but the mental experiences, which these directly symbolize, are the same for all, as also are those things of which our experiences are the images."
> — Aristotle, *On Interpretation*, Ch 1

### The Word of the Heart

Explaining the Incarnation through the most ordinary act of speech, Augustine finds an analogy already present in every conversation: an inward thought becomes outward sound without ceasing to be itself or suffering any change in its own nature, just as the Divine Word became flesh while remaining, in its own nature, unchanged.

> "Just as when we speak, in order that what we have in our minds may enter through the ear into the mind of the hearer, the word which we have in our hearts becomes an outward sound and is called speech; and yet our thought does not lose itself in the sound, but remains complete in itself, and takes the form of speech without being modified in its own nature by the change: so the Divine Word, though suffering no change of nature, yet became flesh, that He might dwell among us."
> — Augustine, *On Christian Doctrine*, Bk I, Ch 13

## 1c. The things of nature functioning symbolically: the book of nature

*[Full section text](1c%20The%20things%20of%20nature%20functioning%20symbolically%20-%20the%20book%20of%20nature/1c%20The%20things%20of%20nature%20functioning%20symbolically%20-%20the%20book%20of%20nature.md)*

### Things That Are Also Signs

Augustine's foundational distinction in *Christian Doctrine*: a "thing" and a "sign" are not mutually exclusive categories. Some objects are things and nothing more, but others — a particular piece of wood, a particular stone, a particular animal — remain ordinary things in themselves while also, on a given occasion, pointing beyond themselves to something further. This double status is the organizing principle of the whole section: nature's contents don't stop being what they are in order to mean something else.

> "Not, however, the wood which we read Moses cast into the bitter waters to make them sweet... nor the ram which Abraham offered up instead of his son; for these, though they are things, are also signs of other things."
> — Augustine, *Christian Doctrine*, BK I, CH 2

### Sign as Evidentiary Inference

Aristotle's logical account of what a sign is in argument: not a mystical correspondence but a demonstrative or generally-approved premise that licenses an inference from one thing's presence to another's — his example is inferring pregnancy from lactation. This grounds the section's more fanciful material (omens, portents, physiognomy) in a sober theory of how a natural fact can count as evidence at all.

> "A sign means a demonstrative proposition necessary or generally approved: for anything such that when it is another thing is, or when it has come into being the other has come into being before or after, is a sign of the other's being or having come into being."
> — Aristotle, *Prior Analytics*, BK II, CH 27

### Cosmic Symbolism

Kepler's Christianized Pythagoreanism: the physical structure of the universe is deliberately laid out to mirror doctrinal and psychological realities, down to precise geometric detail — the three regions of a sphere correspond to the three Persons of the Trinity, and the sun's relation to the planets corresponds to the heart's relation to the rest of the body. For Kepler, nature is symbolic not loosely or poetically but by exact design.

> "...there are three regions, symbols of the three persons of the Holy Trinity - the centre, a symbol of the Father; the surface, of the Son; and the intermediate space, of the Holy Ghost."
> — Kepler, *Epitome*, BK IV

### Portents at Birth and Death

The Shakespearean convention, dramatized across several history plays, that extraordinary natural events — comets, earthquakes, withering trees, bloody-looking moons — cluster around the birth or death of the great, as though nature itself takes editorial notice of political rupture. The device treats the natural world as a kind of chronicler, marking history's turning points with portents legible to anyone watching.

> "The bay-trees in our country are all wither'd, And meteors fright the fixed stars of heaven; The pale-faced moon looks bloody on the earth... These signs forerun the death or fall of kings."
> — Shakespeare, *Richard II*, Act II, Sc IV

### Skepticism Toward Astrological Signs

Edmund's soliloquy supplies the section's sharpest counter-argument: reading personal misfortune off the stars is a self-serving fiction, a way of relocating the blame for one's own faults onto "the sun, the moon, and the stars." Shakespeare places this rebuttal in the mouth of his most calculating villain, which only sharpens it as critique of the very portent-reading dramatized elsewhere in the history plays.

> "This is the excellent foppery of the world, that, when we are sick in fortune — often the surfeits of our own behaviour — we make guilty of our disasters the sun, the moon, and the stars... An admirable evasion of whoremaster man, to lay his goatish disposition to the charge of a star!"
> — Shakespeare, *King Lear*, Act I, Sc II

### Signs, Not Causes

Berkeley's idealist reworking of natural philosophy: what we take to be causal connections in nature — fire causing pain, motion causing sound — are really nothing more than a fixed correspondence, a language of "marks or signs" instituted by God for our instruction. On this view, the task of natural science is to read the vocabulary of that language correctly, not to uncover hidden mechanical causes behind it.

> "The connexion of ideas does not imply the relation of cause and effect, but only of a mark or sign with the thing signified. The fire which I see is not the cause of the pain I suffer upon my approaching it, but the mark that forewarns me of it."
> — Berkeley, *Human Knowledge*, Sect 65

### Mantic Interpretation

Hegel's account of how the Greeks specifically related to nature: unlike symbolic systems where meaning is felt to belong to the object itself, the interpreting seer (μάντις) actively supplies the significance of a natural occurrence — rustling oak leaves, the noise of subterranean water. Meaning does not simply radiate from nature; it is projected onto it by the questioning, comprehending human spirit.

> "The interpretation and explanation of nature and its transformations, the indication of their sense and import, is the act of the subjective spirit; and to this the Greeks attached the name μαντεία."
> — Hegel, *Philosophy of History*, Part II

### Nature as Pasteboard Mask

Ahab's philosophy, and arguably the most extreme statement of the section's whole theme: every visible object in nature is a thin shell concealing some "unknown but still reasoning thing" behind it, and the proper response to a natural sign is not calm reading but violent penetration — to strike through the appearance in search of whatever really moves it.

> "All visible objects, man, are but as pasteboard masks. But in each event — in the living act, the undoubted deed — there, some unknown but still reasoning thing puts forth the mouldings of its features from behind the unreasoning mask. If man will strike, strike through the mask!"
> — Melville, *Moby Dick*, Ch 36

## 1d. The distinction between natural and conventional signs

*[Full section text](1d%20The%20distinction%20between%20natural%20and%20conventional%20signs/1d%20The%20distinction%20between%20natural%20and%20conventional%20signs.md)*

### The Arbitrary Imposition of Words

Locke's clean statement of linguistic conventionalism: words are naturally suited to the general work of signifying — any articulate sound could serve — but no particular word is naturally tied to its particular idea, which is proven by the sheer existence of multiple languages, since a genuinely natural connection between a sound and a meaning would be the same for everyone.

> "Thus we may conceive how words, which were by nature so well adapted to that purpose, came to be made use of by men as the signs of their ideas; not by any natural connexion that there is between particular articulate sounds and certain ideas, for then there would be but one language amongst all men; but by a voluntary imposition, whereby such a word is made arbitrarily the mark of such an idea."
> — Locke, *Human Understanding*, Bk III, Ch 2, §1

### The Confusion of Tongues

Scripture's own account of why conventional signs proliferate and diverge in the first place: a single original human language is deliberately shattered by God as a check on human ambition, so that linguistic diversity itself — the very fact that no sign is naturally tied to its meaning across all peoples — is built into the world's history as a punishment.

> "Come, let us go down, and confuse their language there, so that they will not understand one another's speech." ... "Therefore it was called Babel, because there the LORD confused the language of all the earth."
> — Genesis 11:7, 9

### The Cry of Nature

Rousseau's candidate for the one truly natural, as opposed to conventional, human utterance: an instinctive cry wrung out only by urgent need — danger, pain — and useless for the calmer business of ordinary life, which is why mankind had to invent gesture and then arbitrary articulate sound, by a "common consent" Rousseau finds almost impossible to explain in purely natural terms.

> "The first language of mankind, the most universal and vivid, in a word the only language man needed, before he had occasion to exert his eloquence to persuade assembled multitudes, was the simple cry of nature."
> — Rousseau, *Discourse on Inequality*

### Flexible Speech as the Mark of Reason

Descartes's proposed test for telling genuine sign-use from mere mechanism: a machine, or a beast, might be built to produce a fixed response to a fixed stimulus, but only a reasoning being can rearrange signs freely enough to answer appropriately to anything at all that might be said to it — making the flexibility of sign-use, not sign-use as such, the true mark of an underlying mind.

> "The first is, that they could never use speech or other signs as we do when placing our thoughts on record for the benefit of others... it never happens that it arranges its speech in various ways, in order to reply appropriately to everything that may be said in its presence, as even the lowest type of man can do."
> — Descartes, *Discourse on Method*, Part V

### Naming by Nature vs. by Convention

The classical opening statement of this whole debate: Cratylus holds that a correctly given name is not an arbitrary label but a kind of likeness or picture of the thing it names, so that some names can simply be wrong, against the rival view — which Socrates ultimately favors — that correctness in naming is nothing but agreement, able to signify equally well through unlike sounds as through like ones.

> "Representation by likeness, Socrates, is infinitely better than representation by any chance sign."
> — Plato, *Cratylus*

### The Natural Origin of Language

The Epicurean materialist alternative to any account crediting language to a divine instructor or a deliberate first legislator: nature itself drove early humans to vocal expression exactly as it drives a calf to butt before its horns exist or a puppy to bare unformed teeth — instinctive and bodily, no more requiring an inventor than an animal's growl or bark does.

> "Nature impelled them to utter the various sounds of the tongue and use struck out the names of things, much in the same way as the inability to speak is seen in its turn to drive children to the use of gestures, when it forces them to point with the finger at the things which are before them."
> — Lucretius, *On the Nature of Things*, Bk V

### Natural Sign vs. Conventional Sign

The section's own governing vocabulary, laid down as a formal definition: a natural sign — smoke indicating fire, a footprint indicating a passing animal, an angry face betraying anger — signifies without any intention of signifying at all, while a conventional sign is deliberately given by one mind to draw a thought across into another.

> "Now some signs are natural, others conventional. Natural signs are those which, apart from any intention or desire of using them as signs, do yet lead to the knowledge of something else, as, for example, smoke when it indicates fire."
> — Augustine, *Christian Doctrine*, Bk II, Ch 1

### Psammetichus's Language Experiment

A concrete ancient test of whether any language could be purely natural rather than learned: an Egyptian king raises two infants in total silence to see what word they utter unprompted, hoping to find humanity's original tongue — and gets an existing foreign word instead, *becos*, Phrygian for "bread," which settles a dispute about which nation is oldest rather than proving any word natural to the human tongue as such.

> "He took two children of the common sort, and gave them over to a herdsman to bring up at his folds, strictly charging him to let no one utter a word in their presence... His object herein was to know, after the indistinct babblings of infancy were over, what word they would first articulate... the children both ran up to him with outstretched arms, and distinctly said 'Becos'... 'becos' was the Phrygian name for bread."
> — Herodotus, *History*, Bk II

### The Satire of Communicating by Things

Swift's comic reductio of any suspicion that words are an inferior, merely conventional substitute for direct contact with reality: the Grand Academy's language reformers, reasoning that "words are only names for things," propose replacing speech entirely with the practice of carrying the actual objects one wishes to discuss and pointing at them — exposing, by absurd literalism, how much conventional signs actually save us.

> "Since words are only names for things, it would be more convenient for all men to carry about them, such things as were necessary to express the particular business they are to discourse on."
> — Swift, *Gulliver's Travels*, Part III

### Words as Conventional Symbols

Aristotle's foundational two-tier account, opening the treatise *On Interpretation*: spoken and written words are symbols — conventional, and therefore different from language to language — but what they symbolize, the "mental experiences" or thoughts themselves, and the things those thoughts are of, are the same for every human being regardless of what language is spoken.

> "Spoken words are the symbols of mental experience and written words are the symbols of spoken words. Just as all men have not the same writing, so all men have not the same speech sounds, but the mental experiences, which these directly symbolize, are the same for all, as also are those things of which our experiences are the images."
> — Aristotle, *On Interpretation*, Ch 1

## 1e. The invention of non-verbal symbols: money, titles, seals, ceremonies, courtesies

*[Full section text](1e%20The%20invention%20of%20non%20verbal%20symbols%20money%2C%20titles%2C%20seals/1e%20The%20invention%20of%20non%20verbal%20symbols%20money%2C%20titles%2C%20seals.md)*

### Bodily Imitation

In a digression within the *Cratylus* on how a voiceless humanity might still communicate, Socrates argues that gesture is not arbitrary but naturally mimetic — the very shape of a sign (hands raised for lightness, hands dropped for heaviness) resembles the thing it signifies, so that the body, like the voice, can only ever express something by imitating it.

> "Suppose that we had no voice or tongue, and wanted to communicate with one another, should we not, like the deaf and dumb, make signs with the hands and head and the rest of the body?... We should imitate the nature of the thing; the elevation of our hands to heaven would mean lightness and upwardness; heaviness and downwardness would be expressed by letting them drop to the ground..."
> — Plato, *Cratylus*, 104d-105a

### Civil Honour

Hobbes first catalogs an exhaustive natural grammar of honoring gestures — obeying, gift-giving, deference, praise — then adds a second, artificial tier peculiar to commonwealths: a sovereign can simply designate anything at all as a sign of honor by fiat, so that a title or office becomes honorable purely because the sovereign's will made it so.

> "In Commonwealths where he or they that have the supreme authority can make whatsoever they please to stand for signs of honour, there be other honours. A sovereign doth honour a subject with whatsoever title, or office, or employment, or action that he himself will have taken for a sign of his will to honour him."
> — Hobbes, *Leviathan*, Part I

### Honor as a Sign of Excellence

Arguing that happiness cannot consist in honor, Aquinas defines along the way what honor actually is: not a possession or a reward in itself, but a sign — an outward attestation that other people have recognized some genuine excellence already present in the person honored, rather than a thing that confers the excellence.

> "Honor is given to a man on account of some excellence in him; and consequently it is a sign and attestation of the excellence that is in the person honored."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I-II, Q 2, A 2

### Human Institutions

Sorting out which everyday conventions a Christian may use without superstition, Augustine names an entire class of ordinary non-verbal symbols — clothing that marks sex or rank, and above all the stamping and weighing of coins — as "human institutions": signs that hold force only because people within a given nation have tacitly agreed to honor them, which is exactly why the same signs differ from nation to nation.

> "Among the convenient and necessary arrangements of men with men are to be reckoned whatever differences they choose to make in bodily dress and ornament for the purpose of distinguishing sex or rank... and the arrangements as to weights and measures, and the stamping and weighing of coins, which are peculiar to each state and people... Now these, if they were not devices of men, would not be different in different nations..."
> — Augustine, *On Christian Doctrine*, Bk II, Ch 25

### Idol Ceremony

Alone before Agincourt, Henry V strips away every royal trapping in turn — crown, scepter, sword, robe, title — and finds nothing beneath them but "ceremony": a self-admittedly empty form that manufactures awe and obedience in other men while giving the king wearing it no more ease than any private soldier enjoys.

> "And what have kings that privates have not too, Save ceremony, save general ceremony? And what art thou, thou idol ceremony?... Art thou aught else but place, degree, and form, Creating awe and fear in other men..."
> — Shakespeare, *Henry V*, Act IV, Sc I

### Money as Convention

Working out how goods as unlike as a house and a pair of shoes can be fairly exchanged, Aristotle identifies money as the invented common measure that makes them commensurable at all — and roots its very Greek name in the fact that it is a creature of law and agreement rather than of nature, which is also why a community retains the power to devalue or abolish it.

> "Money has become by convention a sort of representative of demand; and this is why it has the name 'money' (νóμισμα) — because it exists not by nature but by law (νóμος) and it is in our power to change it and make it useless."
> — Aristotle, *Ethics*, Bk V, Ch 5

### Money by the Consent of Men

Locke locates the invention of money at the historical hinge between a state of nature bounded by what a person could actually use and a world of unequal accumulation: gold and silver do almost nothing to sustain life, so their entire value — and the disproportionate possession that follows from it — exists only because people came to a tacit agreement to treat them as precious.

> "Gold, silver, and diamonds are things that fancy or agreement hath put the value on, more than real use and the necessary support of life."
> — Locke, *Civil Government*, Ch V, Sect 46

### Outward Shows

Choosing among Portia's caskets, Bassanio reasons his way past gold and silver by distrusting exactly the kind of symbol this section catalogs: ornament, title, and outward mark routinely dress up a rotten interior — legal pleas, religious error, and simple cowardice all borrow some outward badge of virtue that has nothing to do with what actually lies beneath it.

> "So may the outward shows be least themselves. The world is still deceived with ornament... There is no vice so simple but assumes Some mark of virtue on his outward parts... Thus ornament is but the guilèd shore To a most dangerous sea..."
> — Shakespeare, *Merchant of Venice*, Act III, Sc II

## 1f. Natural signs as the source of meaning in conventional signs: thought as the medium through which words signify things

*[Full section text](1f%20Natural%20signs%20as%20the%20source%20of%20meaning%20in%20conventional%20signs%20-%20thought%20as%20the%20medium%20through%20which%20words%20signify%20things/1f%20Natural%20signs%20as%20the%20source%20of%20meaning%20in%20conventional%20signs%20-%20thought%20as%20the%20medium%20through%20which%20words%20signify%20things.md)*

### Words as Symbols of Mental Experience

Aristotle's founding statement of the whole section's thesis: spoken words are conventional and vary by language, yet what they symbolize — the mental experience itself — does not vary. A word is therefore a sign at one remove: it signifies a thought, and the thought in turn is a natural likeness of the thing, which is why people who share no language whatsoever still share the same underlying mental life.

> "Spoken words are the symbols of mental experience and written words are the symbols of spoken words. Just as all men have not the same writing, so all men have not the same speech sounds, but the mental experiences, which these directly symbolize, are the same for all, as also are those things of which our experiences are the images."
> — Aristotle, *Interpretation*, CH 1

### The Natural-Fitness Theory of Names

Socrates's opening position in the *Cratylus*: before there was voice at all, people wanting to communicate would have imitated things bodily — raising the hands to mean lightness, dropping them to mean heaviness — and vocal naming is simply this same imitative instinct transferred to sounds, so that particular letters (rho for motion, lambda for smoothness) are held to naturally resemble the qualities they name.

> "We should imitate the nature of the thing; the elevation of our hands to heaven would mean lightness and upwardness; heaviness and downwardness would be expressed by letting them drop to the ground."
> — Plato, *Cratylus*

### Convention Completes Resemblance

By the end of the same dialogue, Socrates walks the theory back: the letter lambda, supposedly expressive of softness, turns up in a word for hardness anyway, and the two of them can still understand each other perfectly well. What actually carries the meaning across, he concludes, is not likeness of sound to thing but shared custom — resemblance may have started language, but agreement is what sustains it.

> "The signification of words is given by custom and not by likeness, for custom may indicate by the unlike as well as by the like... Custom and convention must be supposed to contribute to the indication of our thoughts."
> — Plato, *Cratylus*

### The Word of the Heart

Aquinas's account of an inner, unspoken word that precedes and grounds every spoken one: the very act of understanding something produces, within the mind, a conception of the thing understood — and it is this silent mental conception, not the sound itself, that the audible word merely goes on to express. Aquinas uses this psychological structure as the model for how the divine Word (the Son) proceeds from the Father's understanding.

> "Whenever we understand, by the very fact of understanding there proceeds something within us, which is a conception of the object understood, a conception issuing from our intellectual power and proceeding from our knowledge of that object. This conception is signified by the spoken word; and it is called the word of the heart signified by the word of the voice."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 27, A 1

### The Language Test for Thought

Descartes's proposed criterion for distinguishing a genuine mind from a mere mechanism, animal included: not the ability to produce words — a parrot manages that — but the ability to arrange words appropriately in response to whatever is said, on any subject whatever. Only a thinking being behind the words can improvise that way; everything else is disposition of organs, however lifelike the output sounds.

> "It is evident that magpies and parrots are able to utter words just like ourselves, and yet they cannot speak as we do, that is, so as to give evidence that they think of what they say."
> — Descartes, *Discourse on Method*, Part V

### Ideas as the Medium Between Words and Things

Locke's empiricist version of the section's thesis: a word does not attach to a thing directly, but to an abstract idea in the mind, and that idea sits between the word and the thing it is ultimately about. Both the accuracy of our knowledge and the intelligibility of our speech depend on this middle term doing its job — on our ideas actually corresponding, on one side to the things, and on the other to the customary meaning of the name.

> "This abstract idea, being something in the mind, between the thing that exists, and the name that is given to it; it is in our ideas that both the rightness of our knowledge, and the propriety and intelligibleness of our speaking, consists."
> — Locke, *Human Understanding*, BK II, CH XXXII

### Language as the Source of Abstract Ideas

Berkeley's reversal of the picture Locke and the scholastics share: philosophers don't first form abstract general ideas and then invent words to label them; rather, the practical need for economical naming — one word standing in for indefinitely many particular things — is itself what creates the illusion that a single, precise abstract idea must exist to justify the single name.

> "If there had been no such things as speech or universal signs there never had been any thought of abstraction."
> — Berkeley, *Human Knowledge*, Intro, Sect 18

### The Primacy of Words Among Signs

Augustine's survey of every channel by which people signal their thoughts to one another — nods, gestures, military flags, the sound of a trumpet — concludes that words hold a unique and irreplaceable rank among them. Every other kind of sign can be translated into words and explained that way, but the reverse is never possible: no combination of gestures or flags could ever capture what a sentence says.

> "For among men words have obtained far and away the chief place as a means of indicating the thoughts of the mind... I have been able to put into words all those signs, the various classes of which I have briefly touched upon, but I could by no effort express words in terms of those signs."
> — Augustine, *Christian Doctrine*, BK II, CH 3

## 2a. The first and second imposition of words

*[Full section text](2a%20The%20first%20and%20second%20imposition%20of%20words%20names%20signifying/2a%20The%20first%20and%20second%20imposition%20of%20words%20names%20signifying.md)*

### Names of Names

Hobbes's term for what scholastic logicians called "second imposition": most words name things in the world, or our ideas of them, but a special class of words — *universal*, *equivocal*, *syllogism*, *affirmation*, *interrogation* — name nothing in nature at all. Their whole business is to classify or describe other words and forms of speech, making them, in effect, names of names.

> "Fourthly, we bring into account, consider, and give names, to names themselves, and to speeches: for general, universal, special, equivocal, are names of names. And affirmation, interrogation, commandment, narration, syllogism, sermon, oration, and many other such are names of speeches."
> — Hobbes, *Leviathan*, Part I, 57b

### Names of Fancies

Before reaching names of names, Hobbes first isolates a subtler category still within first-order naming: words for neither the external body itself nor for other words, but for our own private sensory impression of a thing — the color, sound, or "idea of it in the fancy" that registers in the mind when something is seen or heard. This class shows Hobbes carving out room, within ordinary naming, for words that point inward at our own perceiving rather than outward at the world.

> "We bring into account the properties of our own bodies, whereby we make such distinction: as when anything is seen by us, we reckon not the thing itself, but the sight, the colour, the idea of it in the fancy... and such are names of fancies."
> — Hobbes, *Leviathan*, Part I, 57b

### The Copula as Custom, Not a Thing

Hobbes's deflationary account of the verb "is": far from naming some mysterious essence or substantial form lurking in things, "is" is itself only the name of a certain form of speech — it signifies nothing more than that two names (such as "man" and "body") happen to be names of one and the same thing. Some languages, he notes, get by with no such word at all, simply placing two names in order; whatever force "is" carries comes from custom, not from naming any real entity.

> "As when one saith, 'a man is a body,' he intendeth that the name of body is necessarily consequent to the name of man... for the placing of two names in order may serve to signify their consequence, if it were the custom (for custom is it that gives words their force)..."
> — Hobbes, *Leviathan*, Part IV, 270a-b

## 2b. The first and second intention of names: words signifying things and words signifying idea

*[Full section text](2b%20The%20first%20and%20second%20intention%20of%20names-%20words%20signifying%20things%20and%20words%20signifying%20idea/2b%20The%20first%20and%20second%20intention%20of%20names-%20words%20signifying%20things%20and%20words%20signifying%20idea.md)*

### First and Second Intention

Aquinas's foundational distinction, borrowed from logic: a "first intention" name like *man* or *animal* signifies a real nature directly. A "second intention" name like *genus* or *species* signifies something else entirely — not a nature, but the logical status the mind confers on that nature once it has grasped it as common to many individuals. The two kinds of name look grammatically alike but point in completely different directions.

> "The names of genera and species, as man or animal, are given to signify the common natures themselves, but not the intentions of those common natures, signified by the terms genus or species."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 29, A 4

### Universality as an Added Intention

Aquinas locates exactly where second intention comes from: a nature such as humanity exists only in this or that individual man, never as such in reality — but when the intellect grasps that nature apart from any individual's particular conditions, the resulting object of thought acquires a further property, universality, that belongs to it only as thought, not as a thing in the world.

> "Humanity which is understood is only in this or that man, but that humanity is apprehended without the individual conditions, that is, that it be abstracted and consequently considered as universal, happens to humanity according as it is perceived by the intellect."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 85, A 2

### Names of Names

Hobbes's own three-tier taxonomy of words, which maps neatly onto first and second intention without using the scholastic vocabulary: some words name things, some name our private mental images of things, and a third class — "universal," "plural," "singular," "definition," "syllogism" — names not things or images at all, but *other words* and forms of speech.

> "Of which words, some are the names of the things conceived... others are the names of the imaginations themselves... and others again are names of names, or of different sorts of speech; as universal, plural, singular, are the names of names."
> — Hobbes, *Leviathan*, Part IV

### The Chimera Eating Second Intentions

Rabelais's send-up of scholastic excess: a mock book-title poses the "most subtle question" of whether a chimera humming in a vacuum could eat second intentions — a joke that only lands because the doctrine was real and, to Renaissance humanists, ripe for mockery as empty hairsplitting divorced from anything in the world.

> "Quæstio Subtilissima, utrum Chimæra in vacuo bombinans possit comedere secundas intentiones" [On the most subtle question: whether a Chimæra, humming in the Void, is able to eat Second Intentions]... those sly intentions of the mind, which in the school are called second notions.
> — Rabelais, *Gargantua and Pantagruel*, BK II / BK III

### Words Signify Ideas, Not Things Directly

Locke's empiricist recasting of the whole distinction: a word's immediate, proper signification is never the thing itself but the speaker's own idea of it — however incomplete that idea may be. A child who has only noticed a shining yellow color calls a peacock's tail "gold," because the word tracks his idea, not the metal's actual properties.

> "Words, in their primary or immediate signification, stand for nothing but the ideas in the mind of him that uses them, how imperfectly soever or carelessly those ideas are collected from the things which they are supposed to represent."
> — Locke, *Human Understanding*, BK III, CH II

### Universals as Creatures of the Understanding

Locke's nominalist verdict on second intention itself: generality is not a feature discoverable in things, since every existing thing is strictly particular. "General" and "universal" name something the mind does — a capacity it confers on a sign to stand for many particulars at once — not anything the sign, or the things it stands for, possess on their own.

> "General and universal belong not to the real existence of things; but are the inventions and creatures of the understanding, made by it for its own use... their general nature being nothing but the capacity they are put into, by the understanding, of signifying or representing many particulars."
> — Locke, *Human Understanding*, BK III, CH III

### Genus and Species Names Arise from Generalization

Lavoisier, borrowing from Condillac, grounds the abstract logical distinction in ordinary child development: a child first applies "tree" to one particular tree (first intention), then to the next similar thing, and by the time he's applied it many times over, the same word has quietly become the name of a class — an abstract idea comprehending all trees (second intention) — with no separate act of definition required.

> "A child... is taught to give the name tree to the first one which is pointed out to him. The next one he sees presents the same idea, and he gives it the same name... till at last the word tree, which he first applied to an individual, comes to be employed by him as the name of a class or a genus, an abstract idea, which comprehends all trees in general."
> — Lavoisier, *Elements of Chemistry*, Preface

### The Fallacy of Naming Thought by Its Object

James's psychological diagnosis of a mistake this whole distinction is meant to prevent: because ordinary language has no separate vocabulary for the *act* of thinking as opposed to the *object* thought about, people quietly assume the thinking must have whatever properties its object has — so an abstract object is assumed to require an equally abstract, freestanding "idea," when in fact the thought and its object belong to entirely different orders.

> "Naming our thought by its own objects, we almost all of us assume that as the objects are, so the thought must be. The thought of several distinct things can only consist of several distinct bits of thought, or 'ideas'; that of an abstract or universal object can only be an abstract or universal idea."
> — James, *Psychology*

## 2c. Intrinsic and extrinsic denominations

*[Full section text](2c%20Intrinsic%20and%20extrinsic%20denominations-%20the%20naming%20of%20things%20according%20to%20their%20natures%20or%20by%20reference%20to%20their%20relations/2c%20Intrinsic%20and%20extrinsic%20denominations-%20the%20naming%20of%20things%20according%20to%20their%20natures%20or%20by%20reference%20to%20their%20relations.md)*

### Absolute and Relative Words

Locke draws the section's central distinction in its plainest form: an "absolute" word like *black* or *thirsty* signifies only something actually present in the thing itself, while a "relative" word like *father* or *king* names the thing by something lying outside it altogether — a comparison to another thing entirely, without which the word would have nothing to attach to.

> "Relation being the considering of one thing with another which is extrinsical to it, it is evident that all words that necessarily lead the mind to any other ideas than are supposed really to exist in that thing to which the words are applied are relative words... a man, black, merry, thoughtful, thirsty, angry, extended; these and the like are all absolute, because they neither signify nor intimate anything but what does or is supposed really to exist in the man thus denominated; but father, brother, king, husband, blacker, merrier, &c., are words which, together with the thing they denominate, imply also something else separate and exterior to the existence of that thing."
> — Locke, *Human Understanding*, Bk II, Ch XXV, Sect 10

### Adam's Naming

The section's founding case of a name given purely by intrinsic authority over a thing's nature, with no relational qualification attached: God brings each animal to Adam not to be classified or compared, but simply to be called whatever Adam calls it, and that act alone fixes what it is named.

> "So out of the ground the LORD God formed every animal of the field and every bird of the air, and brought them to the man to see what he would call them; and whatever the man called every living creature, that was its name. The man gave names to all cattle, and to the birds of the air, and to every animal of the field; but for the man there was not found a helper as his partner."
> — Genesis 2:19-20

### Named for an Intrinsic Quality

Aristotle distinguishes two entirely different grounds on which the same word can be applied: a thing may be called "good" because goodness is a quality actually residing in it, as it does in justice or courage, or it may be called "good" only because it produces or conduces to something good elsewhere — a naming that says nothing about the thing's own intrinsic character at all.

> "We must not merely say that justice and courage are called ‘good’ in one sense, and that what conduces to vigour and what conduces to health are called so in another, but also that the former are so called because of a certain intrinsic quality they themselves have, the latter because they are productive of a certain result and not because of any intrinsic quality in themselves."
> — Aristotle, *Topics*, Bk I, Ch 15

### No Self-Existent Thing

Voicing the Heraclitean extreme that Socrates and Theaetetus are examining, this passage denies intrinsic denomination any foothold at all: if nothing has a fixed nature and everything is only ever "becoming and in relation," then even a name as basic as "man" or "stone" cannot truly fix anything — every name would, in the end, be extrinsic.

> "There is no one self-existent thing, but everything is becoming and in relation... in the language of nature all things are being created and destroyed, coming into being and passing into new forms; nor can any name fix or detain them..."
> — Plato, *Theaetetus*, 520b

### The Column on the Right

Aquinas's classic illustration of pure extrinsic denomination: a column becomes "on the right" only because an animal happens to stand beside it in a certain way, so the column can gain and lose the name without anything in the column itself changing at all. He applies this directly to names like "Lord," which are truly said of God only because of a change in creatures, not in God.

> ""On the right" is not applied to a column unless it stands as regards an animal on the right side, which relation is not really in the column but in the animal... there is nothing to prevent these names which import relation to the creature from being predicated of God temporally, not by reason of any change in Him, but by reason of the change of the creature; as a column is on the right of an animal without change in itself, but by the shifting of the animal."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 13, A 7

### The Strasburg Clock

Locke's complication of intrinsic naming: even when we believe we are naming a natural substance — gold, a sheep, a plant — by its own inner nature, we are never actually in contact with that hidden "real essence" at all, only with the outward qualities it happens to present to us, the way an ordinary observer of a great clock sees only its face and hands, never the works that drive them.

> "Our faculties carry us no further towards the knowledge and distinction of substances, than a collection of those sensible ideas which we observe in them; which, however made with the greatest diligence and exactness we are capable of, yet is more remote from the true internal constitution from which those qualities flow... than, as I said, a countryman's idea is from the inward contrivance of that famous clock at Strasburg, whereof he only sees the outward figure and motions."
> — Locke, *Human Understanding*, Bk III, Ch VI, Sect 9

### Universality as Relation

Arguing against Locke's own theory of abstract general ideas, Berkeley relocates the generality of a name like "triangle" out of any special intrinsic content the idea would have to possess, and into something purely relational instead: a particular idea becomes universal only by being made to stand for, and represent, a whole class of other particulars.

> "Universality, so far as I can comprehend, not consisting in the absolute, positive nature or conception of anything, but in the relation it bears to the particulars signified or represented by it; by virtue whereof it is that things, names, or notions, being in their own nature particular, are rendered universal."
> — Berkeley, *Human Knowledge*, Intro, Sect 15

## 2d. Proper and common names

*[Full section text](2d%20Proper%20and%20common%20names/2d%20Proper%20and%20common%20names.md)*

### A Magic Bias

Walter Shandy's private theory, played for comedy but argued with total seriousness: a proper name is never a neutral tag attached after the fact, but an active force that reaches into a person's character and conduct from the moment it is given — which is exactly why he considers the accidental christening of his son Tristram a catastrophe.

> "That was in respect to the choice and imposition of christian names, on which he thought a great deal more depended than what superficial minds were capable of conceiving. His opinion, in this matter, was, That there was a strange kind of magic bias, which good or bad names, as he called them, irresistibly impressed upon our characters and conduct."
> — Sterne, *Tristram Shandy*

### Names for Every Sheep

Locke argues that a purely proper vocabulary — one distinct name for every particular thing — is not merely impractical but doubly disqualified: human memory could never hold that many distinct names in the first place, and even if it could, a name known only to its coiner could never do language's real job, which is making one mind's ideas known to another.

> "It is impossible that every particular thing should have a distinct peculiar name... we may easily find a reason why men have never attempted to give names to each sheep in their flock, or crow that flies over their heads... If it were possible, it would yet be useless; because it would not serve to the chief end of language... the names of them could not be significant or intelligible to another, who was not acquainted with all those very particular things which had fallen under my notice."
> — Locke, *Human Understanding*, Bk III, Ch III, Sect 2-3

### Nothing Universal but Names

Hobbes states the section's core distinction as flatly as it can be stated, then draws the nominalist conclusion that follows from it: a proper name like "Peter" belongs to one thing only, a common name like "man" belongs to many, but nothing in the world outside language is itself actually universal — every real thing named is a single, individual particular.

> "Of names, some are proper, and singular to one only thing; as Peter, John, this man, this tree: and some are common to many things; as man, horse, tree; every of which, though but one name, is nevertheless the name of diverse particular things; in respect of all which together, it is called an universal, there being nothing in the world universal but names; for the things named are every one of them individual and singular."
> — Hobbes, *Leviathan*, Part I

### One Oak Called A, Another Called B

Against Locke, Rousseau imagines the reverse historical order: the very first language, he argues, must have been made of nothing but proper names, one for every individual object, because early humans could grasp that two things differed long before they could grasp what the two things had in common — a common name is the late, hard-won achievement, not the starting point.

> "Every object at first received a particular name without regard to genus or species, which these primitive originators were not in a position to distinguish; every individual presented itself to their minds in isolation... If one oak was called A, another was called B; for the primitive idea of two things is that they are not the same, and it often takes a long time for what they have in common to be seen: so that, the narrower the limits of their knowledge of things, the more copious their dictionary must have been."
> — Rousseau, *Inequality*

### Primary and Secondary Substance

Aristotle grounds the whole distinction metaphysically before it is ever a distinction about words: an individual man is a substance in the truest sense, complete and needing no further subject to inhere in, while "man" and "animal" are substances only in a secondary, derivative sense, as the species and genus that gather primary substances into groups — which is exactly why the first kind of thing gets a proper name and the second kind gets a common one.

> "Substance, in the truest and primary and most definite sense of the word, is that which is neither predicable of a subject nor present in a subject; for instance, the individual man or horse. But in a secondary sense those things are called substances within which, as species, the primary substances are included... For instance, the individual man is included in the species ‘man’, and the genus to which the species belongs is ‘animal’; these, therefore... are termed secondary substances."
> — Aristotle, *Categories*, Ch 5

### The Child and the Tree

Quoting Condillac to justify his own reform of chemical vocabulary, Lavoisier grounds the class-name in ordinary child development: a child first calls one single tree "tree," extends the same word to the next tree by the same association, and only much later, once real differences among trees matter to him, learns to cut the general name back down with more particular ones — the same movement, Lavoisier argues, that any well-made science of naming must follow.

> ""A child," says the Abbé de Condillac, "is taught to give the name tree to the first one which is pointed out to him... till at last the word tree, which he first applied to an individual, comes to be employed by him as the name of a class or a genus... he will soon learn to distinguish them by specific and particular names." This is the logic of all the sciences and is naturally applied to chemistry."
> — Lavoisier, *Elements of Chemistry*, Preface

### The Heaven of the People

In the traditional etymological mode of medieval hagiography, Chaucer treats Cecilia's own proper name as a text to be read and unpacked, drawing out layered meanings — heaven, lastingness, freedom from blindness, "the heaven of the people" — that are made to say, in miniature, everything her legend will go on to tell at length.

> "Cecile, as I writen finde, Is joyned, by a maner conjoininge Of “hevene” and “Lia”; and heer, in figuringe, The “heven” is set for thought of holinesse, And “Lia” for hir lasting bisinesse... this maydens name bright Of “hevene” and “leos” comth, for which by right Men mighte hir wel “the heven of peple” calle..."
> — Chaucer, *Second Nun's Prologue*

### Whereby the Person Is Distinguished

Asking whether "Father" is truly God the Father's proper name, Aquinas first states a general theory of what any proper name does for any person at all: it signifies the very thing that marks that person off from every other person — which for the first Person of the Trinity is nothing else but paternity itself, making "Father" not a courtesy title but a strict proper name.

> "The proper name of any person signifies that whereby the person is distinguished from all other persons... Now it is paternity which distinguishes the person of the Father from all the other persons. Hence this name Father, whereby paternity is signified, is the proper name of the person of the Father."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 33, A 2

## 2e. Abstract and concrete names

*[Full section text](2e%20%20Abstract%20and%20concrete%20names/2e%20%20Abstract%20and%20concrete%20names.md)*

### Abstract–Concrete Fallacy in the Syllogism

Aristotle's illustration of how a syllogism can go wrong not through faulty logical form but through sloppy choice of terms: substituting an abstract term (*health*) for the concrete term that the reasoning actually requires (*healthy*) can make a false conclusion look as if it follows validly, because the abstract and concrete forms are not simply interchangeable stand-ins for each other.

> "It would seem to follow that health cannot belong to any man. The reason for this is that the terms are not set out well in the statement, since if the things which are in the conditions are substituted, no syllogism can be made, e. g. if ‘healthy’ is substituted for ‘health’ and ‘diseased’ for ‘disease’."
> — Aristotle, *Prior Analytics*, Bk I, Ch 34, 66b-c

### Abstract and Concrete Names of God

Aquinas's rule for how the abstract/concrete distinction, ordinarily used of composite creatures, must be adapted for a God who is absolutely simple: since God has no form distinct from His own subsisting act of being, theologians use abstract names (*Godhead*, *life*) to express His simplicity and concrete names (*God*, *living*) to express that He truly subsists — even though, unlike in creatures, both kinds of names ultimately point to one and the same undivided reality.

> "And as God is simple, and subsisting, we attribute to Him abstract names to signify His simplicity, and concrete names to signify His subsistence and perfection, although both these kinds of names fail to express His mode of being, since our intellect does not know Him in this life as He is."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 13, A 1, Reply Obj. 2

### Concrete Names Stand for the Person

A technical corollary Aquinas draws from the same distinction: because a concrete name like *God* signifies the divine nature as possessed by a subject, it can stand for one of the three divine Persons — which is why "God begot God" is a true statement about Father and Son. An abstract name like *Essence*, by contrast, signifies the form by itself and can never stand for a Person, so the parallel sentence "Essence begets essence" is simply false.

> "The word essence, however, in its mode of signification, cannot stand for Person, because it signifies the essence as an abstract form. Consequently, what properly belongs to the persons whereby they are distinguished from each other cannot be attributed to the essence."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 39, A 5

### Abstract Names as Names of Accidents

Hobbes's deflationary, materialist account of where abstract names come from: when we notice some accident or quality of a body — its being moved, its being hot — we coin a new name for that quality alone by "a little change or wresting" of the concrete name (*hot* becomes *heat*, *moved* becomes *motion*). The abstraction, for Hobbes, is never from matter itself, only from the reckoning, or "account," of matter.

> "and for living put into the account life; for moved, motion; for hot, heat; for long, length, and the like: and all such names are the names of the accidents and properties by which one matter and body is distinguished from another. These are called names abstract, because severed, not from matter, but from the account of matter."
> — Hobbes, *Leviathan*, Part I, 57a

### Abstract Terms Resist Mutual Predication

Locke's logical test for distinguishing genuine abstract ideas from one another: no two abstract terms can ever truly be affirmed of each other, even when their corresponding concrete terms describe the very same subject without contradiction. That "man is an animal, or rational, or white" is unproblematic, but "humanity is animality" is instantly recognized as false, shows for Locke that all real affirmation happens in the concrete.

> "This we see in the common use of language, which permits not any two abstract words, or names of abstract ideas, to be affirmed one of another. For how near of kin soever they may seem to be, and how certain soever it is that man is an animal, or rational, or white, yet every one at first hearing perceives the falsehood of these propositions: humanity is animality, or rationality, or whiteness..."
> — Locke, *Human Understanding*, Bk III, Ch VIII

### The Late Emergence of Abstraction in Language

Rousseau's conjectural history of how naming must have actually developed: because recognizing what several different things have in common is a difficult, gradual achievement, the very first languages could not have had general or abstract terms at all — every individual object first received its own unique proper name, so early vocabularies were far larger and more particular than later, more abstract ones.

> "If one oak was called A, another was called B; for the primitive idea of two things is that they are not the same, and it often takes a long time for what they have in common to be seen: so that, the narrower the limits of their knowledge of things, the more copious their dictionary must have been."
> — Rousseau, *Inequality*, 341b-c

## 3a. Verbal ambiguity: indefiniteness or multiplicity of meaning

*[Full section text](3a%20Verbal%20ambiguity%20indefiniteness%20or%20multiplicity%20of%20meaning/3a%20Verbal%20ambiguity%20indefiniteness%20or%20multiplicity%20of%20meaning.md)*

### Comic Equivocation

Shakespeare's stock device for slapstick misunderstanding: Petruchio orders Grumio to "knock me" at Hortensio's gate, meaning "knock for me," and Grumio, playing dumb, takes it as "strike me" and refuses to knock at the gate until the ambiguity has been wrung for every possible blow. The same doubleness of meaning that undoes Croesus plays here, in a different key, purely for laughs.

> "Knock at the gate! O heavens! Spake you not these words plain, “Sirrah, knock me here, rap me here, knock me well, and knock me soundly”? And come you now with, “knocking at the gate”?"
> — Shakespeare, *Taming of the Shrew*, Act I, Sc II [1-44]

*See also:* **[The Comic Uses of Equivocation](../4b.%20The%20dependence%20of%20demonstration%20on%20univocal%20terms-%20formal%20fallacies%20due%20to%20equivocation/4b%20Glossary.md#the-comic-uses-of-equivocation)** in *4b. The dependence of demonstration on univocal terms* — a different playwright (Sterne, not Shakespeare) working the same device, but there the joke is aimed at Locke's own remedy for equivocation: defining your terms doesn't save Tristram from finding a fresh double meaning.

### Finite Words, Infinite Things

Aristotle's explanation, in the *Sophistical Refutations*, of why ambiguity in language is not an occasional defect but a structural inevitability: because we reason with names rather than with the things themselves, and because names are finite in number while the things they must stand for are infinite, some names are necessarily forced to carry more than one meaning.

> "It is impossible in a discussion to bring in the actual things discussed: we use their names as symbols instead of them; and therefore we suppose that what follows in the names, follows in the things as well, just as people who calculate suppose in regard to their counters... names are finite and so is the sum-total of formulae, while things are infinite in number. Inevitably, then, the same formulae, and a single name, have a number of meanings."
> — Aristotle, *Sophistical Refutations*, Ch 1 [165a5-13]

### Idols of the Market-Place

Bacon's diagnosis, in the *Novum Organum*, of the worst of the four "idols" that mislead the understanding: false impressions bred by ordinary language itself, formed loosely by popular usage and then, in a kind of reversal, governing the very reason that supposedly governs them. His example is "moist," a word smeared thinly enough over water, flame, air, and glass that under examination it turns out to mean nothing precise at all.

> "The idols of the market are the most troublesome of all, those namely which have entwined themselves round the understanding from the associations of words and names. For men imagine that their reason governs words, whilst, in fact, words react upon the understanding; and this has rendered philosophy and the sciences sophistical and inactive."
> — Bacon, *Novum Organum*, Bk I, Aph 59

### Predestined Ambiguity

Freud's extension of the classical theme into dream-analysis: a word, sitting at the junction of many associated ideas, carries what he calls a "predestined ambiguity" that dreams — and neurotic symptoms — exploit deliberately, condensing several thoughts into one ambiguous expression the way a poet finds a single rhyme to carry two meanings at once.

> "A word, as the point of junction of a number of ideas, possesses, as it were, a predestined ambiguity, and the neuroses (obsessions, phobias) take advantage of the opportunities for condensation and disguise afforded by words quite as eagerly as do dreams."
> — Freud, *Interpretation of Dreams* [277d-278a]

### Tests for an Ambiguous Term

Aristotle's practical toolkit, in the *Topics*, for detecting whether a word is genuinely ambiguous rather than merely broad: check whether its contrary splits into several senses, whether its intermediate terms differ, or — most memorably — whether its definitions across different uses share nothing in common, as with "donkey," which names both the animal and a mechanical device, and so is really two different words wearing one sound.

> "Look also at the genera of the objects denoted by the same term, and see if they are different without being subaltern, as (e.g.) ‘donkey’, which denotes both the animal and the engine. For the definition of them that corresponds to the name is different: for the one will be declared to be an animal of a certain kind, and the other to be an engine of a certain kind."
> — Aristotle, *Topics*, Bk I, Ch 15

### The Ambiguous Oracle

The most famous case study in the whole section: the Delphic prophecy that if Croesus attacked Persia he would "destroy a mighty empire," paired with a verse riddle that Media's monarchy would fall to "a mule." Both turned out true in a sense Croesus never anticipated — the empire destroyed was his own, and the "mule" was Cyrus, of mixed royal and common blood — making the episode a permanent example of how oracular language exploits, rather than resolves, multiplicity of meaning.

> "Wait till the time shall come when a mule is monarch of Media; Then, thou delicate Lydian, away to the pebbles of Hermus; Haste, oh! haste thee away, nor blush to behave like a coward."
> — Herodotus, *History*, Bk I [11b-d]

*See also:* **[The Ambiguous Oracle](../5e%20The%20interpretation%20of%20the%20word%20of%20God/5e%20Glossary.md#the-ambiguous-oracle)** in *5e. The interpretation of the word of God* — the same Croesus story, read there not as a specimen of multiplicity of meaning but as a case study in whose fault the ambiguity is: Herodotus's own verdict is that Croesus should have asked which empire was meant.

### The Cloudy Medium of Language

Madison's argument, in defending the necessarily imprecise wording of the newly drafted Constitution, that verbal obscurity is not a failure of drafting skill but a condition of language itself — so pervasive, he notes, that not even divine communication is exempt: even "the Almighty himself," addressing mankind in its own language, has his meaning rendered "dim and doubtful" by the medium of words.

> "But no language is so copious as to supply words and phrases for every complex idea, or so correct as not to include many equivocally denoting different ideas... When the Almighty himself condescends to address mankind in their own language, his meaning, luminous as it must be, is rendered dim and doubtful by the cloudy medium through which it is communicated."
> — *The Federalist*, No. 37 (Madison)

### The Doubtful Syllable

Montaigne's example of how much can ride on a single ambiguous word: the Eucharistic disputes of his own age over the one syllable "Hoc" in *hoc est corpus meum* ("this is my body"), which he offers as proof that grammar, not doctrine alone, is what "creates most disturbance in the world."

> "Our speaking has its failings and defects, as well as all the rest: grammar is that which creates most disturbance in the world... How many quarrels, and those of how great importance, has the doubt of the meaning of this syllable Hoc created in the world?"
> — Montaigne, *Essays* [253c-254a]

### The Imperfection of Words

Locke's chapter-length diagnosis of why language is inherently leaky: since sounds bear no natural connection to ideas but signify only by "arbitrary imposition," any doubtfulness in what a word means traces back not to the sound itself but to the complexity, or instability, of the idea a speaker attaches to it — a problem he judges worst of all for moral words like justice or gratitude.

> "...since sounds have no natural connexion with our ideas, but have all their signification from the arbitrary imposition of men, the doubtfulness and uncertainty of their signification... has its cause more in the ideas they stand for than in any incapacity there is in one sound more than in another to signify any idea..."
> — Locke, *Human Understanding*, Bk III, Ch IX

### The Same Word in Contrary Senses

Augustine's rule for reading Scripture: the same figurative image can signify opposite things depending on where it appears — the lion is both Christ, in "the lion of the tribe of Judah," and the devil, "as a roaring lion." Rather than treating this as a flaw, Augustine treats it as a feature of figurative language that the careful interpreter must learn to track case by case, never assuming one fixed meaning.

> "...a lion stands for Christ in the place where it is said, “The lion of the tribe of Judah hath prevailed”; and again, stands for the devil where it is written, “Your adversary the devil, as a roaring lion, walketh about seeking whom he may devour.”"
> — Augustine, *Christian Doctrine*, Bk III, Ch 25

### The Sophism of "Learn"

The eristic brothers Euthydemus and Dionysodorus build a philosophical trap on a single equivocal verb: do those who learn, learn what they know, or what they do not know? Because "learn" can mean either "come newly to know" or "exercise knowledge already possessed" (as when a pupil "learns" a dictation), any answer the boy Cleinias gives can be turned against him — a textbook demonstration of how a logical trap can ride on nothing more than an unnoticed shift in a word's sense.

> "Do those, said he, who learn, learn what they know, or what they do not know?"
> — Plato, *Euthydemus*, 65a-84a,c

### Words Colored by the Speaker's Passions

Hobbes's account of why the names of virtues and vices can never ground sound reasoning: such names carry not only the nature of the thing named but also the disposition of the speaker, so that the same conduct gets a different name depending on who is doing the praising or the blaming.

> "...for one man calleth wisdom what another calleth fear; and one cruelty what another justice; one prodigality what another magnanimity; and one gravity what another stupidity, etc."
> — Hobbes, *Leviathan*, Part I [57d-58a]

## 3b. The distinction between univocal and equivocal speech

*[Full section text](3b.%20The%20distinction%20between%20univocal%20and%20equivocal%20speech/3b.%20The%20distinction%20between%20univocal%20and%20equivocal%20speech.md)*

### Univocal and Equivocal Naming

Aristotle's foundational definition, on which the whole topic rests: things are named *equivocally* when they share only a name while its definition differs for each — a real man and a painted figure are both called "animal," but the definition of "animal" fits only one of them properly. Things are named *univocally* when both the name and its complete definition apply identically to each — a man and an ox are both "animal" in exactly the same sense.

> "Things are said to be named ‘equivocally’ when, though they have a common name, the definition corresponding with the name differs for each... things are said to be named ‘univocally’ which have both the name and the definition answering to the name in common."
> — Aristotle, *Categories*, Ch 1, [1a1-12] 5a

### Analogical Predication

Aquinas's third option between the two extremes: since no name applies to God and creatures in exactly the same sense (ruling out univocal use), but pure equivocation would make it impossible to reason from creatures to God at all, names like "wise" or "good" must be predicated *analogically* — a single term expressing genuinely different but proportionally related meanings anchored to one reference point, the way "healthy" names both the cause of health (medicine) and its sign (urine) without meaning quite the same thing in each case.

> "Some things are said of God and creatures analogically, and not in a purely equivocal nor in a purely univocal sense... thus healthy applied to urine signifies the sign of animal health, and applied to medicine signifies the cause of the same health."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 13, A 5

### Pure Equivocation

Spinoza's illustration of the opposite extreme from analogy — two uses of a word sharing nothing at all but the sound. He argues that if God's intellect and will truly differ from ours in their whole essence and existence, as his metaphysics requires, then applying "intellect" and "will" to both God and man is equivocation of the purest kind, with no more real likeness between the two uses than between a star-cluster and a dog that barks.

> "The intellect and will which could constitute His essence would have to differ entirely from our intellect and will, and could resemble ours in nothing except in name. There could be no further likeness than that between the celestial constellation of the Dog and the animal which barks."
> — Spinoza, *Ethics*, Part I, Prop 17, Schol.

### Verbal Disputes

Locke's diagnosis of how unnoticed equivocation manufactures fake disagreements: two people arguing over whether a bat is a bird are not actually disputing any fact about bats, since neither doubts what a bat really is or what qualities it has — they are silently applying different, unexamined definitions of the word "bird." Once both sides agree explicitly on what the name covers, the dispute simply evaporates.

> "Whether a bat be a bird or no, is not a question, Whether a bat be another thing than indeed it is, or have other qualities than indeed it has... Were they agreed in the signification of these two names, it were impossible they should dispute about them."
> — Locke, *Human Understanding*, Bk III, Ch XI, Sect 7

### Equivocal Strictures

Sterne's comic dramatization of the same danger in a bawdy key. Because the narrator of *Tristram Shandy* never precisely fixes what he means by "Nose," his readers are free to supply their own second, ruder sense — a running joke the book turns into a mock-serious plea for exact definition, staged here as Eugenius catching a word caught between two roads of meaning.

> "Here are two senses, cried Eugenius... here are two senses—quoth he—And here are two roads, replied I, turning short upon him—a dirty and a clean one—which shall we take?——The clean, by all means, replied Eugenius."
> — Sterne, *Tristram Shandy*, Ch 31, 307b-308b

### Unequivocal Abstract Numbers

James extends the univocal/equivocal distinction into arithmetic itself: only the abstract numbers we hold fixed as mental, ideal objects are truly unequivocal — one plus one simply means two, by definition. Concrete physical things are not reliably "unequivocally numbered" at all, since a drop of water added to a drop of water yields one drop, not two; only artificial constancies like atoms let us force nature's shifting countability into line with arithmetic's fixed meanings.

> "Only the abstract numbers themselves are unequivocal, only those which we create mentally and hold fast to as ideal objects always the same. A concrete natural thing can always be numbered in a great variety of ways."
> — James, *Psychology*, 875b-876a

## 3c(1). The same word used literally and figuratively: metaphors

*[Full section text](3c%20%281%29%20The%20same%20word%20used%20literally%20and%20figuratively-%20metaphors/3c%281%29%20The%20same%20word%20used%20literally%20and%20figuratively-%20metaphors.md)*

### The Abuse of Metaphor in Reasoning

Hobbes's materialist objection to metaphor as a tool of philosophy: motion and bodies are real, physical things, and only the *words* describing them can be stretched figuratively — so when the Schoolmen speak of "metaphorical motion" to cover a case where nothing has actually moved, they mistake a permissible looseness of language for an impossible looseness of fact.

> "The Schools find in mere appetite to go, or move, no actual motion at all; but because some motion they must acknowledge, they call it metaphorical motion, which is but an absurd speech; for though words may be called metaphorical, bodies and motions cannot."
> — Hobbes, *Leviathan*, Part I, Ch 6

### The Anagram as Hidden Second Sense

Swift's Grand Academy of Lagado literalizes the fantasy that a word secretly contains another meaning inside itself: the anagrammatist doesn't infer a hidden sense from context, but physically breaks a sentence's letters apart and reassembles them into an unrelated, seditious message — a parody of figurative reading that treats the very letters of "the same word" as mechanically hiding their own opposite.

> "If I should say in a letter to a friend, 'Our brother Tom has just got the piles,' a man of skill in this art would discover how the same letters which compose that sentence may be analysed into the following words; 'Resist,—a plot is brought home—the tour.'"
> — Swift, *Gulliver's Travels*, Part III, Ch 6

### Bondage to Signs

Augustine names a failure distinct from ordinary literalism: not just misreading a figurative passage as literal, but becoming so fixed on the sign itself — the word, the rite, the letter — that the mind never lifts itself to what the sign was made to point toward at all.

> "Now it is surely a miserable slavery of the soul to take signs for things, and to be unable to lift the eye of the mind above what is corporeal and created, that it may drink in eternal light."
> — Augustine, *Christian Doctrine*, Bk III, Ch 5

*See also:* **[Bondage to Signs](../5c%20The%20symbolism%20of%20the%20sacraments%20and%20of%20sacramental%20or%20ritualistic%20acts/5c%20Glossary.md#bondage-to-signs)** in *5c. The symbolism of the sacraments* — Augustine applies this same principle two chapters later to a specific case: the Old Testament's ceremonial law as a *useful* bondage to true signs, unlike the Gentiles' bondage to signs of nothing at all.

### Equivocation

The opening definition of Aristotle's Categories, and the master concept this entire chapter of the Syntopicon elaborates: two things are named equivocally when they share a name but not the definition that name expresses — a real man and a painted man are both called "animal," yet no single account of "animal" fits them both.

> "Things are said to be named 'equivocally' when, though they have a common name, the definition corresponding with the name differs for each."
> — Aristotle, *Categories*, Ch 1

### The Etymological Drift of a Word

Gibbon traces "pagan" through a chain of small, literal-to-figurative extensions — a shared village fountain, to the rural neighborhood around it, to civilians as against soldiers, to non-Christians as against the "soldiers of Christ" — showing how a word can travel a great historical distance from its origin through a sequence of steps that were each, in isolation, a reasonable metaphorical extension.

> "Πάγη, in the Doric dialect... signifies a fountain; and the rural neighbourhood which frequented the same fountain derived the common appellation of pagus and pagans... By an easy extension of the word, pagan and rural became almost synonymous... The Christians were the soldiers of Christ; their adversaries who refused his sacrament, or military oath of baptism, might deserve the metaphorical name of pagans."
> — Gibbon, *Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire*, note 173

### The Heart as Sun as King

Harvey opens his physiological treatise not with anatomy but with a dedication built on a triple proportional analogy — heart is to body as sun is to cosmos as king is to kingdom — the same logical structure Aristotle assigns to proportional metaphor, here deployed as literal political flattery in the front matter of a work of experimental science.

> "The heart of animals is the foundation of their life, the sovereign of everything within them, the sun of their microcosm, that upon which all growth depends, from which all power proceeds. The King, in like manner, is the foundation of his kingdom, the sun of the world around him, the heart of the republic, the fountain whence all power, all grace doth flow."
> — Harvey, *Motion of the Heart*, dedication to Charles I

### The Letter Kills, the Spirit Gives Life

The scriptural maxim (2 Corinthians 3:6) that Augustine returns to across both excerpted works as the governing rule for reading any text whose words might be meant figuratively: cling only to the literal sense and the same words can become false, absurd, or morally monstrous; read for the spirit beneath the letter and they become true.

> "With joy I heard Ambrose in his sermons to the people oftentimes most diligently recommend this text for a rule, 'The letter killeth, but the Spirit giveth life'; whilst he drew aside the mystic veil, laying open spiritually what according to the 'letter' seemed to teach something unsound."
> — Augustine, *Confessions*, Bk VI, par. 6

### Metaphor by Proportion (Analogy)

Aristotle's technical account of the most sophisticated of the four kinds of metaphor: given four terms where the second stands to the first as the fourth stands to the third, either extreme may be substituted for the other — evening can be called "the old age of the day" because old age stands to life exactly as evening stands to day.

> "As old age (D) is to life (C), so is evening (B) to day (A). One will accordingly describe evening (B) as the 'old age of the day'... and old age (D) as the 'evening' or 'sunset of life'."
> — Aristotle, *Poetics*, Ch 21

### Metaphor in Scripture as Accommodation to Sense

Aquinas's answer to why Holy Scripture teaches through metaphor rather than abstraction: since all human knowledge begins in the senses, spiritual truths reach the mind more naturally when clothed in the likeness of material things, and this method serves everyone — learned and simple alike — rather than only those capable of pure intellectual contemplation.

> "It is befitting Holy Writ to put forward divine and spiritual truths under the likenesses of material things. For God provides for everything according to the capacity of its nature. Now it is natural to man to attain to intellectual truths through sensible things, because all our knowledge originates from sense."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 1, A 9

### Words Transferred from Sense to Mind

Locke's empiricist thesis about the deep structure of language itself: even our most abstract vocabulary for purely mental operations was not coined fresh but borrowed — figuratively, at the origin of every language — from words that first named ordinary sensible things, so that "spirit" literally means breath and "angel" literally means messenger before either comes to mean something incorporeal.

> "Spirit, in its primary signification, is breath; angel, a messenger: and I doubt not but, if we could trace them to their sources, we should find, in all languages, the names which stand for things that fall not under our senses to have had their first rise from sensible ideas."
> — Locke, *Human Understanding*, Bk III, Ch 1, §5

## 3c(2). The same word used with varying degrees of generality and specificity

*[Full section text](3c%20%282%29%20The%20same%20word%20used%20with%20varying%20degrees%20of%20generality%20and/3c%282%29%20The%20same%20word%20used%20with%20varying%20degrees%20of%20generality%20and.md)*

### Justice in the Wide and Narrow Sense

Aristotle's example of a single word carrying two nested meanings at once: "justice" in the wide sense names lawful conduct as a whole — everything virtue requires toward one's neighbor — while "justice" in the narrow, particular sense names only one specific virtue within that whole, the one opposed to grasping more than one's fair share.

> "There is, then, another kind of injustice which is a part of injustice in the wide sense, and a use of the word ‘unjust’ which answers to a part of what is unjust in the wide sense of ‘contrary to the law’."
> — Aristotle, *Ethics*, Bk V, Ch 2, 376a-378c

### The Rule of Species and Genus

Tichonius's fourth interpretive rule, as Augustine relays it: a scriptural passage that begins by speaking of a narrow "species" (a single city, a single nation) will often, without any signal, broaden without warning into the wider "genus" of which that species is only a part (all nations, the whole Church) — so a reader who keeps applying the passage only to its original narrow subject misses what it more fully means.

> "When Scripture, having up to a certain point been speaking about the species, makes a transition at that point from the species to the genus, the reader must then be carefully on his guard against seeking in the species what he can find much better and more surely in the genus."
> — Augustine, *Christian Doctrine*, Bk III, Ch 34

### Words Extended by Custom

Aquinas's general account of how a word's meaning broadens over time: a term first coined for one strict, primary sense — "sight," naming only the act of the eye — gets extended by ordinary usage to a wider range of things it resembles or serves ("see how it tastes"), and eventually even to purely intellectual vision, so that the same word remains correct in either its narrow original sense or its broadened customary one.

> "Any word may be used in two ways—that is to say, either in its original application or according to custom. This is clearly shown in the word “sight,”... extended in common speech to all knowledge obtained through the other senses... In its primary meaning it signifies that which makes manifest to the sense of sight; afterwards it was extended to that which makes manifest to knowledge of any kind."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 67, A 1

### The Ambiguity of Money

Smith's example of a word carrying two related but distinct meanings without anyone noticing the slippage: "money" sometimes means strictly the metal coins themselves, and sometimes carries an unstated further reference to the goods those coins can buy — and conflating the two senses, he argues, has led economists to seriously misjudge what a nation's wealth, or an individual's income, actually consists in.

> "It is the ambiguity of language only which can make this proposition appear either doubtful or paradoxical... when we say that a man is worth fifty or a hundred pounds a year, we mean commonly to express not only the amount of the metal pieces which are annually paid to him, but the value of the goods which he can annually purchase or consume."
> — Smith, *Wealth of Nations*, Bk II, 123b-d

### The Narrow, Popular Sense of "Utility"

Mill's complaint about how his own technical term got corrupted in ordinary use: the public narrowed "utilitarian" into a crabbed rejection of beauty, ornament, and pleasure, when the doctrine's actual, broader technical sense — running from Epicurus to Bentham — identifies utility with pleasure itself, not its opposite.

> "Those who know anything about the matter are aware that every writer, from Epicurus to Bentham, who maintained the theory of utility, meant by it, not something to be contradistinguished from pleasure, but pleasure itself, together with exemption from pain..."
> — Mill, *Utilitarianism*, Ch 2, 447b-d

### The Narrowing of a General Word to a Particular Thing

Freud's etymological case of meaning moving the other direction — narrowing rather than broadening: the Latin *materia* (matter or material in general, sharing a root with *mater*, "mother") narrows over time into the Portuguese *madeira*, meaning specifically "wood" — supplying Freud a linguistic explanation for why wood so often symbolizes woman or mother in dreams.

> "This would be an instance of a process which is by no means rare, in that a general name for material has come finally to be applied to a particular material only... this madeira is merely a modified form of the Latin materia, which again signifies material in general."
> — Freud, *General Introduction*, 509d

## 3c(3). The same word used to signify an attribute and its cause or effect

*[Full section text](3c%20%283%29%20The%20same%20word%20used%20to%20signify%20an%20attribute%20and%20its%20cause%20of/3c%283%29%20The%20same%20word%20used%20to%20signify%20an%20attribute%20and%20its%20cause%20of.md)*

### "Good" as Intrinsic Quality vs. Productive Result

Aristotle's own methodological instruction for handling a word with several senses: it is not enough to note that "good" applies both to virtues like justice and to things like healthy food — one must explain *why* the senses differ. Justice and courage are called good because of a quality intrinsic to them; food and medicine are called good only because they produce a further, separate result — the same word naming an attribute in one case and naming whatever causes that attribute in the other.

> "We must not merely say that justice and courage are called ‘good’ in one sense, and that what conduces to vigour and what conduces to health are called so in another, but also that the former are so called because of a certain intrinsic quality they themselves have, the latter because they are productive of a certain result and not because of any intrinsic quality in themselves."
> — Aristotle, *Topics*, Bk I, Ch 15, [106a1-9] 149d

### Focal Meaning

Aristotle's broader theory covering the whole pattern: a term like "being," or his standing example "healthy," is not used in one identical sense of everything it names, nor merely ambiguously either — every use instead relates back to one central, defining case. Food is "healthy" because it preserves health, medicine because it produces health, a complexion because it is a symptom of health: different relations, but all pointing to a single focus.

> "There are many senses in which a thing may be said to ‘be’, but all that ‘is’ is related to one central point, one definite kind of thing, and is not said to ‘be’ by a mere ambiguity. Everything which is healthy is related to health, one thing in the sense that it preserves health, another in the sense that it produces it, another in the sense that it is a symptom of health, another because it is capable of it."
> — Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, Bk IV, Ch 2, [1003a33-b11] 522b

### "Healthy" Applies Primarily to the Animal

Aquinas draws out the order of priority hidden inside this pattern: when several things share a name because of their different relations to one primary instance, that primary instance belongs in the very definition of all the others. "Healthy" said of medicine means "the cause of health in the animal"; "healthy" said of urine means "the sign of the animal's health" — so both secondary uses can only be defined by first mentioning the animal's own health, never the reverse.

> "Healthy applied to animals comes into the definition of healthy applied to medicine, which is called healthy as being the cause of health in the animal, and also into the definition of healthy which is applied to urine, which is called healthy in so far as it is the sign of the animal's health."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 13, A 6

### Denomination from a Common Term

Aquinas applies the identical causal pattern to an entirely different case: the word "sacrament." Just as an animal, medicine, diet, and urine can all be called "healthy" through four different relations to one health — possessing it, causing it, preserving it, or signifying it — several different things can likewise be called "sacrament" through different relations to one sanctity, though in the strict theological sense only the sign-relation among these counts as a sacrament proper.

> "All things that are ordered to one, even in different ways, can be denominated from it; thus, from health which is in an animal, not only is the animal said to be healthy through being the subject of health, but medicine also is said to be healthy through producing health, diet through preserving it, and urine, through being a sign of health."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part III, Q 60, A 1

### One Truth Reflected in Many

Aquinas extends the same cause-and-sign pattern to truth itself. In one sense there are as many truths as there are created intellects that grasp something correctly; in another sense there is only a single Truth, since every created truth is caused by, and exists only as a reflection of, the one divine intellect from which the others take their name — much as urine is called "healthy" only by reflecting the animal's own health.

> "As from one man’s face many likenesses are reflected in a mirror, so many truths are reflected from the one divine truth."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 16, A 6

## 3d. The significance of names predicated of heterogeneous things: the analogical as intermediate between the univocal and the equivocal

*[Full section text](3d.%20The%20significance%20of%20names%20predicated%20of%20heterogeneous/3d.%20The%20significance%20of%20names%20predicated%20of%20heterogeneous.md)*

### Being Said in Many Ways (Focal Meaning)

Aristotle's doctrine that "being" is not one uniform predicate but is said differently of substance, quality, quantity, and the other categories — and yet these senses are not simply unrelated (equivocal), because all of them refer back to the primary sense, substance. Everything else "is" only because it is a determination of some substance; this focal relationship is the model for how a single name can span genuinely heterogeneous kinds of thing without either meaning exactly one thing or meaning arbitrarily many.

> "There are several senses in which a thing may be said to 'be'... in one sense the 'being' meant is 'what a thing is' or a 'this', and in another sense it means a quality or quantity or one of the other things that are predicated as these are... And all other things are said to be because they are, some of them, quantities of that which is in this primary sense, others qualities of it... Clearly then it is in virtue of this category that each of the others also is."
> — Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, BK VII, CH 1

### Ambiguous Terms Detected by Their Contraries

Aristotle's diagnostic method, in the *Topics*, for catching a hidden equivocation: check whether a term's contrary, or its intermediate, or the genus it falls under splits apart under the different uses. If "sharp" opposes "dull" for a blade but opposes "flat" for a musical note, that mismatch is proof that "sharp" itself is not one single sense stretched across two subjects, but two different senses wearing one name.

> "The contrary of 'sharp' in the case of a note is 'flat', while in the case of a solid edge it is 'dull'. Clearly, then, the contrary of 'sharp' bears several meanings, and if so, so also does 'sharp'."
> — Aristotle, *Topics*, BK I, CH 15

### Analogical Sameness Across Heterogeneous Domains

Aristotle's account of how causes and principles can be "the same" across domains as unrelated as medicine and architecture — not because the same thing literally recurs in both, but because each domain has its own version of the same pattern (a state aimed at, a privation of it, a material substrate, and a productive art that moves the material toward the state). Medicine and building-craft share nothing in content, yet share this relational structure exactly.

> "Health, disease, body; the moving cause is the medical art. Form, disorder of a particular kind, bricks; the moving cause is the building art."
> — Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, BK XII, CH 4

### "Good" Said in as Many Senses as "Being"

Aristotle's argument against Plato's Form of the Good: since "good" is predicated across every category — of God and reason (substance), of the virtues (quality), of due measure (quantity), of the useful (relation), of the right moment (time) — it cannot possibly name one single universal thing common to all of them, on pain of being reducible to a single category instead of ranging freely across all of them the way "being" itself does.

> "'Good' has as many senses as 'being' (for it is predicated both in the category of substance, as of God and of reason, and in quality, i.e. of the virtues, and in quantity, i.e. of that which is moderate, and in relation, i.e. of the useful...); clearly it cannot be something universally present in all cases and single."
> — Aristotle, *Ethics*, BK I, CH 6

### Order of Analogates

Aquinas's formal account of analogical predication: unlike univocal terms, whose instances are all equally instances of the common notion, analogical terms rank their instances — one sense is primary and gets written into the definition of all the others, which are secondary and derivative. "Healthy" said of an animal is primary; "healthy" said of medicine or urine only makes sense by reference back to that animal's health, as its cause or its sign respectively.

> "In all names which are said of many in an analogical sense, they must all be said with reference to one thing, and therefore this one thing must be placed in the definition of them all... Healthy applied to animals comes into the definition of healthy applied to medicine, which is called healthy as being the cause of health in the animal, and also into the definition of healthy which is applied to urine, which is called healthy in so far as it is the sign of the animal's health."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 13, A 6

### Degrees of Likeness

Aquinas's taxonomy of what "like" can mean, running from the strongest sense down to the weakest: two things sharing a form to the same degree (perfect likeness), sharing it to different degrees (imperfect likeness), an effect resembling its cause only generically (as sunlit things resemble the sun) — and finally, when the cause is outside every genus altogether, no shared form at all, only a bare relational analogy. This last, thinnest rung is exactly where creatures stand in relation to God.

> "If there is an agent not contained in any genus, its effects will still more distantly reproduce the form of the agent, not... so as to participate in the likeness of the agent's form according to the same specific or generic aspect, but only according to some sort of analogy; as being is common to all."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 4, A 3

### The Ambiguity of "Substance"

Locke's challenge to scholastic metaphysics: the word "substance" gets applied to God, to finite spirits, and to bodies as though it were obviously one notion — but if it really is used in exactly the same sense each time, that has the absurd consequence that God, souls, and rocks would differ only as different "modifications" of one common stuff, the way a tree and a pebble are both just modified matter.

> "I desire those who lay so much stress on the sound of these two syllables, substance, to consider whether applying it, as they do, to the infinite, incomprehensible God, to finite spirits, and to body, it be in the same sense... God, spirits, and body, agreeing in the same common nature of substance, differ not any otherwise than in a bare different modification of that substance."
> — Locke, *Human Understanding*, BK II, CH XIII

### Being Is Not a Real Predicate

Kant's demolition of the ontological argument, extending the section's Aristotelian premise (that "being" is not a genus alongside other genera) into a flat denial that existence adds any content to a concept at all. "Is" merely posits the subject with all its predicates already attached; it never enlarges the description of a thing the way an actual predicate — a color, a size, a quality — would.

> "Being is evidently not a real predicate, that is, a conception of something which is added to the conception of some other thing. It is merely the positing of a thing, or of certain determinations in it. Logically, it is merely the copula of a judgement... A hundred real dollars contain no more than a hundred possible dollars."
> — Kant, *Pure Reason*

## 4a. The relation between univocal meaning and definition

*[Full section text](4a.%20The%20relation%20between%20univocal%20meaning%20and%20definition/4a.%20The%20relation%20between%20univocal%20meaning%20and%20definition.md)*

### Agreement on the Name vs. the Definition

The Stranger's methodological warning at the start of the hunt for the Sophist: two people can share a word perfectly and still mean different things by it, because agreement about a name is not agreement about the thing. Real univocal communication requires working out a definition together — which is why the dialogue rehearses the method first on something easy (an angler) before risking it on something hard (a sophist).

> "We ought always to come to an understanding about the thing itself in terms of a definition, and not merely about the name minus the definition."
> — Plato, *Sophist*

### Univocal: Same Name, Same Definition

Aristotle's technical definition of the section's key term: two things are univocal with respect to a name not merely when the same word applies to both, but when the very definition that explains the word applies to both without change. Genus, species, and differentiae are predicated of their subjects univocally in exactly this sense — the definition transfers along with the name.

> "The word 'univocal' was applied to those things which had both name and definition in common."
> — Aristotle, *Categories*, CH 5

### A Definition Must Identify Its Own Subject

Among Aristotle's diagnostic tests for a bad definition: if the wording alone, detached from the term it supposedly defines, doesn't make clear what is being defined, the definition has failed at its basic job. He compares this to old paintings so stylized that without a caption naming the figures, no one could tell what — or whom — they depicted.

> "See if, when it is merely stated by itself, it is not evident what it defines: just as in the works of the old painters, unless there were an inscription, the figures used to be unrecognizable."
> — Aristotle, *Topics*, BK VI, CH 2

### Against Dichotomous Division

Aristotle's biological case against defining a species by a chain of successive either/or splits (Footed/Footless, Feathered/Featherless...): the method inevitably tears apart natural kinds that share several independent traits at once — ants fall on both sides of Feathered/Featherless, since some fly and some don't — because no single differentia, however qualified by its predecessors, can capture what a whole essence actually is.

> "We must define at the outset by a multiplicity of differentiae... No single differentia, I repeat, either by itself or with its antecedents, can possibly express the essence of a species."
> — Aristotle, *Parts of Animals*, BK I, CH 2–3

### The Notion Expressed by a Name Is Its Definition

Aquinas's compact formula, offered while explaining how any name at all can be applied to God: a name's meaning just is its definition — which is why even a name we can rightly apply to God without seeing His essence still fails to define Him the way "man" defines the essence of man, since we possess no such comprehensive account of the divine nature to serve as its definition.

> "For the notion expressed by the name is the definition."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 13, A 1

### Definitions as the Beginning of Reckoning

Hobbes locates the entire reliability of geometry — "the only science it hath pleased God hitherto to bestow on mankind" — in one discipline: settling what every term means before reasoning with it. Anyone who skips this step and reasons from loosely fixed words gets caught in their own language, struggling only to become more entangled, like a bird in birdlime.

> "A man that seeketh precise truth had need to remember what every name he uses stands for... or else he will find himself entangled in words, as a bird in lime twigs; the more he struggles, the more belimed. And therefore in geometry... men begin at settling the significations of their words; which settling of significations, they call definitions."
> — Hobbes, *Leviathan*, Part I

### Nominal vs. Real Definition

Pascal's crucial distinction: a purely nominal definition just assigns an arbitrary new name to something already clearly pointed out ("call every number divisible into two equal parts *even*"), and being arbitrary, it can never be contradicted. But the moment a word already has an accepted meaning and someone claims that meaning *is* some further thing — "time is the motion of a created thing" — that is no longer a definition at all but a disputable proposition wearing a definition's disguise.

> "It is not the nature of these things that I say is known by all; it is simply the relation between the name and the thing... For definitions are made only to point out the things named and not to reveal their nature."
> — Pascal, *Geometrical Demonstration*, Section 1

### A Definition Fixes Use, Not a Single Idea

Berkeley, arguing against the theory of abstract general ideas, points out that a rigorous definition doesn't actually pin a word to one determinate mental picture. "Triangle" is defined without specifying size, color, or the particular angles of the sides — so an enormous variety of distinct particular triangles all satisfy the same univocal definition equally well.

> "It is one thing for to keep a name constantly to the same definition, and another to make it stand everywhere for the same idea; the one is necessary, the other useless and impracticable."
> — Berkeley, *Human Knowledge*, Intro, Sect 18

### Three Sources of Definitional Obscurity

Madison's application of the section's problem to law: even the most carefully drafted statute remains equivocal until litigation and precedent gradually pin down its meaning, because obscurity in any definition can arise from three independent directions — the object itself may be indistinct, the mind conceiving it may be imperfect, or language, the medium carrying the thought across to another mind, may simply be inadequate to the task.

> "Here, then, are three sources of vague and incorrect definitions: indistinctness of the object, imperfection of the organ of conception, inadequateness of the vehicle of ideas."
> — *The Federalist*, Number 37

### Undefined Terms Cannot Rank Things

Darwin, wrestling with whether human races should count as separate species, points out that the whole question is unanswerable so long as "species" itself has no generally accepted definition — and that trying to settle it without one is exactly as hopeless as trying to decide, with no definitions of the terms at all, whether a given cluster of houses is a village, a town, or a city.

> "It is a hopeless endeavour to decide this point, until some definition of the term 'species' is generally accepted... We might as well attempt without any definition to decide whether a certain number of houses should be called a village, town, or city."
> — Darwin, *Descent of Man*

## 4b. The dependence of demonstration on univocal terms: formal fallacies due to equivocation

*[Full section text](4b.%20The%20dependence%20of%20demonstration%20on%20univocal%20terms-%20formal%20fallacies%20due%20to%20equivocation/4b.%20The%20dependence%20of%20demonstration%20on%20univocal%20terms-%20formal%20fallacies%20due%20to%20equivocation.md)*

### Univocal Middle Term

Aristotle's positive requirement, underlying the whole section: a demonstration does not need Platonic Forms or some mysterious "One beside a Many," but it does need at least this much — a single term predicated in exactly the same sense of every individual it covers. Strip that away and the middle term of a syllogism dissolves, and with it the possibility of demonstration at all.

> "We conclude, then, that there must be a single identical term unequivocally predicable of a number of individuals."
> — Aristotle, *Posterior Analytics*, BK I, CH 11

### Fallacies Dependent on Language

Aristotle's taxonomy, in the *Sophistical Refutations*, of the six ways an argument can look valid while resting on nothing but a slippage of words: ambiguity, amphiboly, combination, division, accent, and form of expression. His stock example trades on the shifting reference of "the sick man" — proof that a single, perfectly ordinary phrase can silently mean two different things within one argument.

> "The same man is both seated and standing and he is both sick and in health... but it is the seated man who stood up, and the sick man who was recovering."
> — Aristotle, *Sophistical Refutations*, CH 4

### Logical Error Kept in Proportion

Epictetus's Stoic corrective to both sophistry and its opposite vice — treating a slip in reasoning as either trivial or catastrophic. Examining premises carefully is a genuine discipline, worth real effort; but mishandling a syllogism is still only a mistake in reasoning, not (his deflating comparison) an act of arson or parricide.

> "'I suppose,' I said, 'that I have burnt the Capitol.' 'Slave,' he replied, 'was the thing omitted here the Capitol?'"
> — Epictetus, *Discourses*, BK I, CH 7

### Analogy as a Mean Between Equivocation and Univocation

Aquinas applies the section's logic directly to theology: words like "wise" cannot be predicated of God and creatures univocally (God's wisdom isn't a limited quality the way a man's is), but they can't be purely equivocal either — if they were, the fallacy of equivocation would poison every argument from creatures to God, and nothing about God could be demonstrated at all. Analogical predication is the narrow path between these two failures.

> "If that were so, it follows that from creatures nothing could be known or demonstrated about God at all, for the reasoning would always fall into the fallacy of equivocation... this mode of community is a mean between pure equivocation and simple univocation."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 13, A 5

### Inconstant Signification

Hobbes's diagnosis of why moral and political language is especially treacherous ground for reasoning: the names of things that please or displease us shift with the passions of whoever is using them, so the very same conduct gets one name from an admirer and another from a critic. Words this unstable, he concludes, can never serve as a genuine foundation for argument.

> "One man calleth wisdom what another calleth fear; and one cruelty what another justice; one prodigality what another magnanimity; and one gravity what another stupidity... And therefore such names can never be true grounds of any ratiocination."
> — Hobbes, *Leviathan*, Part I

### Disputes About Words, Not Things

Locke's field report from a meeting of physicians arguing over whether "liquor" passes through the nerves: suspecting the dispute was merely verbal, he had them pause to define the term first — and the argument evaporated, because each had silently been using "liquor" for a different idea while agreeing almost entirely on the underlying facts.

> "I (who had been used to suspect, that the greatest part of disputes were more about the signification of words than a real difference in the conception of things) desired... they would first examine and establish amongst them, what the word liquor signified... each of them made it a sign of a different complex idea."
> — Locke, *Human Understanding*, BK III, CH IX

### Longevity of Dispute as Evidence of Ambiguity

Hume generalizes Locke's diagnosis into a standing rule: since human minds are basically alike, a controversy that stays unresolved for centuries despite sustained, good-faith argument is itself evidence that the disputants are attaching different ideas to the same words — as he goes on to argue is exactly the case with the age-old quarrel over liberty and necessity.

> "From this circumstance alone, that a controversy has been long kept on foot, and remains still undecided, we may presume that there is some ambiguity in the expression, and that the disputants affix different ideas to the terms employed in the controversy."
> — Hume, *Human Understanding*, Sect VIII

### The Comic Uses of Equivocation

Sterne's parody turns the whole chapter's remedy — Locke's insistence on fixing one's terms before arguing — into a running joke. Tristram solemnly "defines" a persistently double-meaning word with mock precision, demonstrating in the process that a determined equivocator can always find a crevice a strict definition doesn't quite close.

> "'Here are two senses,' cried Eugenius... pointing... to the word Crevice... 'And here are two roads,' replied I... 'a dirty and a clean one — which shall we take?'... For by the word Nose... I declare, by that word I mean a nose, and nothing more, or less."
> — Sterne, *Tristram Shandy*, Ch 31

*See also:* **[Comic Equivocation](../3a%20Verbal%20ambiguity%20indefiniteness%20or%20multiplicity%20of%20meaning/3a%20Glossary.md#comic-equivocation)** in *3a. Verbal ambiguity: indefiniteness or multiplicity of meaning* — the same comic device in Shakespeare rather than Sterne: Grumio's mock-literal "knock at the gate" has no Locke to parody, just a servant playing dumb.

## 4c (1). The nature and utility of semantic analysis

*[Full section text](4c%20%281%29%20The%20nature%20and%20utility%20of%20semantic%20analysis/4c%20%281%29%20The%20nature%20and%20utility%20of%20semantic%20analysis.md)*

### Clearness as the Chief Merit of Language

Opening his *Natural Faculties* by naming the two capacities peculiar to animals, Galen pauses over his own word choice: he could coin a sharper technical vocabulary (splitting "soul" into vegetative and sensory kinds), but deliberately declines to, on the principle that a scientific term's first duty is to be understood, not to be exact for its own sake.

> "We, however, for our part, are convinced that the chief merit of language is clearness, and we know that nothing detracts so much from this as do unfamiliar terms; accordingly we employ those terms which the bulk of people are accustomed to use..."
> — Galen, *Natural Faculties*, Bk I, Ch 1

### Refutations That Depend on Ambiguity

In the *Sophistical Refutations*, Aristotle catalogs the class of fallacious arguments that trade on a term or phrase carrying more than one sense, and prescribes the remedy: rather than merely sensing that something is wrong, the respondent must name which of the several senses is doing the work and show the conclusion holds only under a sense nobody actually intended.

> "Of the refutations, then, that depend upon ambiguity and amphiboly some contain some question with more than one meaning, while others contain a conclusion bearing a number of senses... one should reply with regard to an ambiguity, whether of a term or of a phrase, in this manner, that ‘in one sense it is so, and in another not so’..."
> — Aristotle, *Sophistical Refutations*, Ch 19, 243d-247a

### Resolving Ambiguity by Punctuation and Context

Opening his treatise on how to interpret difficult scriptural passages, Augustine sets out a working method for genuine verbal ambiguity: first rule out an error in punctuation or pronunciation, then test each remaining reading against the established rule of faith, and where more than one reading still survives, let the surrounding context — what comes before and after — settle the choice.

> "But when proper words make Scripture ambiguous, we must see in the first place that there is nothing wrong in our punctuation or pronunciation... it remains to consult the context, both what goes before and what comes after, to see which interpretation, out of many that offer themselves, it pronounces for and permits to be dovetailed into itself."
> — Augustine, *On Christian Doctrine*, Bk III, Ch 2

### Signs Taken for Things

Augustine's diagnosis of the most damaging interpretive error: reading a figurative expression as though it were literal. He treats this as more than a stylistic lapse — mistaking a sign for the reality it signifies is a kind of bondage that traps the mind at the level of the corporeal and keeps it from rising to what the sign actually points toward.

> "We must beware of taking a figurative expression literally. For the saying of the apostle applies in this case too: “The letter killeth, but the spirit giveth life.”... Now it is surely a miserable slavery of the soul to take signs for things, and to be unable to lift the eye of the mind above what is corporeal and created..."
> — Augustine, *On Christian Doctrine*, Bk III, Ch 5

### The Correctness of Names

In the *Cratylus*, Socrates works out a position between Cratylus's naturalism — a name is either exactly right or not a name at all — and pure arbitrary convention. Naming, he argues, is a craft like painting or building: a name is an imitation of its object built letter by letter, and like any imitation it can be executed well or badly, so that some names simply fit their objects better than others.

> "He who by syllables and letters imitates the nature of things, if he gives all that is appropriate will produce a good image, or in other words a name; but if he subtracts or perhaps adds a little, he will make an image but not a good one; whence I infer that some names are well and others ill made."
> — Plato, *Cratylus*, 107c-114a,c

### The Logical Art as the First Measure

Answering an interlocutor impatient with what sounds like mere logic-chopping, Epictetus argues that semantic and logical precision cannot be waved off as a secondary, merely formal concern. Every other kind of inquiry depends on it, the way weighing out grain depends on first checking that the scale itself is accurate.

> "This is the reason, I suppose, why they place the logical art first, as in the measuring of corn we place first the examination of the measure. But if we do not determine first what is a modius, and what is a balance, how shall we be able to measure or weigh anything?"
> — Epictetus, *Discourses*, Bk I, Ch 17

### The Many Senses of "Cause"

Book V of the *Metaphysics* works as a philosophical dictionary, running through the several distinct senses in which key terms are actually used before any argument built on them can be trusted. Under "cause" alone, Aristotle distinguishes material, formal, efficient, and final senses, then draws the methodological point explicit: because one word covers several senses, a single thing can truly have several different causes at once, not just one.

> "‘Cause’ means (1) that from which, as immanent material, a thing comes into being, e.g. the bronze is the cause of the statue and the silver of the saucer... These, then, are practically all the senses in which causes are spoken of, and as they are spoken of in several senses it follows both that there are several causes of the same thing, and in no accidental sense..."
> — Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, Bk V, Ch 2, 533a-547d

### Variable Signification

Augustine's caution against treating one discovered symbolic meaning as a fixed key: the same word or image can signify one thing in one passage and its very opposite in another. He illustrates it elsewhere in the chapter with Christ's use of "leaven," spoken as a warning in one place and as a hopeful image of the kingdom in another — so each figurative occurrence has to be read afresh in its own context rather than decoded by a single universal cipher.

> "We are not to suppose there is any rule that what a thing signifies by similitude in one place it is to be taken to signify in all other places."
> — Augustine, *On Christian Doctrine*, Bk III, Ch 25

## 4d. The use of metaphors and myths in science and philosophy

*[Full section text](4d%20The%20use%20of%20metaphors%20and%20myths%20in%20science%20and%20philosophy/4d%20The%20use%20of%20metaphors%20and%20myths%20in%20science%20and%20philosophy.md)*

### Aviary of Knowledge

In the *Theaetetus*, trying to explain how someone who already possesses knowledge could still form a false opinion, Socrates offers an image in place of a definition: the mind as an aviary stocked with birds, each bird a piece of knowledge already caught and owned. Knowing is having caged the bird; erring is reaching in and grasping the wrong one — turning an apparent contradiction (knowing what one does not know) into an ordinary mistake of aim.

> "let us now suppose that in the mind of each man there is an aviary of all sorts of birds— some flocking together apart from the rest, others in small groups, others solitary, flying anywhere and everywhere."
> — Plato, *Theaetetus*, 197

### Chariot of the Soul

Rather than argue directly for the soul's threefold structure, Socrates admits he can only speak of it "briefly, and in a figure": a charioteer struggling to drive a pair of winged horses, one noble and obedient, the other unruly, whose relative mastery determines whether the soul rises toward truth or falls to earth. Plato flags the device itself as a substitute for a demonstration no mortal discourse could give.

> "Of the nature of the soul, though her true form be ever a theme of large and more than mortal discourse, let me speak briefly, and in a figure. And let the figure be composite—a pair of winged horses and a charioteer."
> — Plato, *Phaedrus*, 246

### Flash of Analogy

James locates scientific genius in essentially the same mental act that produces a poet's metaphor: the sudden perception of similarity between remote things. Newton's law and Darwin's natural selection, on this account, both began as an almost poetic flash — an apple's fall recognized as kin to the moon's, artificial breeding recognized as kin to the struggle for existence — available only to minds unusually prone to noticing resemblance.

> "The flash of similarity between an apple and the moon, between the rivalry for food in nature and the rivalry for man's selection, was too recondite to have occurred to any but exceptional minds. Genius, then... is identical with the possession of similar association to an extreme degree."
> — James, *Psychology*, "Different Orders of Human Genius"

### Judgment of Naked Souls

Closing his argument with Callicles not with further argument but with what he calls "a very pretty tale," Socrates describes Zeus discovering that living judges are deceived by the fine bodies and reputations of the living. His fix: strip both the dead and their judges bare, so that a naked soul can be read truly, without the "veil" of clothes, wealth, or rank. Socrates insists on the story's status as something he actually believes, not mere ornament.

> "Listen, then, as story-tellers say, to a very pretty tale, which I dare say that you may be disposed to regard as a fable only, but which, as I believe, is a true tale, for I mean to speak the truth."
> — Plato, *Gorgias*, 523

### Lines of Force

Faraday's device for thinking about magnetism and electricity without committing to any claim about what, physically, actually connects a magnet to the needle it moves. He insists the phrase "line of force" carry only direction, strength, and quantity — remaining deliberately agnostic about mechanism — while noting that pictured this way, the idea has proved more fertile for new results than any rival picture of fluids or centres of action.

> "I desire to restrict the meaning of the term line of force, so that it shall imply no more than the condition of the force in any given place, as to strength and direction; and not to include... any idea of the nature of the physical cause of the phenomena."
> — Faraday, *Researches in Electricity*, ¶3075

### Metaphor as Confessed Deceit

Hobbes bars metaphor from demonstration, counsel, and every "rigorous search of truth," grouping it with outright equivocation — but grants it one relative virtue. Unlike a word quietly twisted to fit a speaker's passions, a metaphor announces its own figurative status up front, which is exactly why using it to argue, rather than merely to illustrate, counts as "manifest folly."

> "In demonstration, in council, and all rigorous search of truth, judgement does all... But for metaphors, they are in this case utterly excluded. For seeing they openly profess deceit, to admit them into council, or reasoning, were manifest folly."
> — Hobbes, *Leviathan*, Part I, 67c

### Natural Selection as Necessary Metaphor

Answering critics who took "selection" to imply conscious choice and "Nature" to imply a deity, Darwin defends his terminology by pointing to identical shorthand already accepted throughout the sciences — astronomers' gravity that "rules" the planets, chemists' "elective" affinities — arguing that such personifying language is not a scientific error but an almost unavoidable economy of expression.

> "It has been said that I speak of natural selection as an active power or Deity; but who objects to an author speaking of the attraction of gravity as ruling the movements of the planets? Every one knows what is meant and is implied by such metaphorical expressions; and they are almost necessary for brevity."
> — Darwin, *Origin of Species*, 40

### Necessity of Metaphor in New Discovery

Confronting phenomena in animal generation for which existing language simply has no words, Harvey weighs his only two options and finds both wanting: borrowed metaphor turns careful description into a riddle the reader cannot solve, while coining brand-new terms only explains one unknown by another — "less to bring a torch to lighten, than to darken things still more with a cloud."

> "if he would have recourse to metaphors, and by means of old and familiar terms would make known his ideas concerning the things he has newly discovered, the reader would have little chance of understanding him better than if they were riddles that were propounded... to have recourse to new and unusual terms were less to bring a torch to lighten, than to darken things still more with a cloud."
> — Harvey, *On Animal Generation*, 336d-337a

### Prometheus and Epimetheus

Asked whether virtue can be taught, Protagoras answers with a myth rather than an argument, judging it "more interesting": Epimetheus's careless distribution of natural gifts left man naked and defenseless, and Prometheus's stolen fire supplied only the practical arts, not political wisdom — which Zeus, fearing mankind's mutual destruction, ordered distributed to every citizen alike, rather than reserved to the few skilled specialists the way medicine or any other art is.

> "I should like them all to have a share; for cities cannot exist, if a few only share in the virtues, as in the arts. And further, make a law by my order, that he who has no part in reverence and justice shall be put to death, for he is a plague of the state."
> — Plato, *Protagoras*, 322 (Zeus, in the myth related by Protagoras)

### Proportional Metaphor

Aristotle treats metaphor not as decoration but as a form of insight — the perception of a likeness between things not obviously related — and its most exact form is proportional (A is to B as C is to D, so that D can be called "the B of C"). He explicitly extends this same power beyond rhetoric and poetry into philosophical thought itself.

> "Metaphors must be drawn... from things that are related to the original thing, and yet not obviously so related—just as in philosophy also an acute mind will perceive resemblances even in things far apart. Thus Archytas said that an arbitrator and an altar were the same, since the injured fly to both for refuge."
> — Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, Bk III, Ch 11

### Reversal of the Cosmos

To correct an error in an earlier definition of the statesman, the Eleatic Stranger unfolds a cosmic myth: the universe alternates between ages when a divine pilot guides its motion directly and ages when he releases the helm, letting the world reverse itself and run down under its own momentum until, threatened with dissolution, God takes hold again. The myth's point is dialectical as much as cosmological — showing that the earlier account had wrongly modeled the human king on the divine shepherd of the other cycle.

> "There is a time when God himself guides and helps to roll the world in its course and there is a time, on the completion of a certain cycle, when he lets go, and the world being a living creature... turns about and by an inherent necessity revolves in the opposite direction."
> — Plato, *Statesman*, 269

### Scripture's Necessary Use of Metaphor

Asking whether Holy Scripture should use metaphors, Aquinas answers yes, and for a reason stronger than mere accessibility: because all human knowledge begins in the senses, spiritual truths can only reach the mind at all if clothed in likenesses of material things. Unlike poetry, where metaphor serves pleasure, in sacred doctrine — Aquinas insists — the device is not ornamental but "necessary and useful."

> "It is befitting Holy Writ to put forward divine and spiritual truths under the likenesses of material things. For God provides for everything according to the capacity of its nature. Now it is natural to man to attain to intellectual truths through sensible things... Hence in Holy Writ spiritual truths are fittingly taught under the metaphors of material things."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 1, A 9

## 4e. The use of signs in reasoning

*[Full section text](4e%20The%20use%20of%20signs%20in%20reasoning/4e%20The%20use%20of%20signs%20in%20reasoning.md)*

### Divination: Artificial vs. Natural

Bacon's taxonomy of the two grounds on which future events are foretold: "artificial" divination reasons from observed signs and tokens to a conclusion — itself splitting into a rational branch (argument from causes) and a merely experimental branch (argument from coincidence, which shades into superstition) — while "natural" divination bypasses signs altogether, arising instead from an unmediated inward presentiment. The distinction marks the outer boundary of the whole topic: only the artificial kind counts as reasoning from signs at all.

> "Divination hath been anciently and fitly divided into artificial and natural: whereof artificial is, when the mind maketh a prediction by argument, concluding upon signs and tokens; natural is, when the mind hath a presention by an internal power, without the inducement of a sign."
> — Bacon, *Advancement of Learning*, Bk II, §11

### Dream as Diagnostic Sign

Pantagruel's theory of dream-interpretation folded into medical semiotics: a violent, disturbed sleep is not a meaningless accident but a legible symptom, sleep's own restorative work on "concoction" pushing a hidden, festering sickness toward the surface where it first becomes perceptible as unease.

> "Know for a certain truth, that every sleep that endeth with a starting, and leaves the person irksome, grieved, and fretting, doth either signify a present evil, or otherwise presageth and portendeth a future imminent mishap."
> — Rabelais, *Gargantua and Pantagruel*, Bk III, Ch 14

### Necessary Sign (Tekmerion)

Aristotle's Rhetoric sorts the loose, ordinary-language word "sign" into two logically distinct kinds: the infallible sign or *tekmerion* — a "complete proof," illustrated by a fever proving illness or lactation proving recent childbirth — and the fallible, unnamed ordinary sign, illustrated by fast breathing as evidence of fever, which remains open to refutation. Only the first kind is genuine, irrefutable demonstration; the second only makes its conclusion probable.

> "The infallible kind is a 'complete proof' (*tekmerion*); the fallible kind has no specific name... 'The fact that he has a fever is a sign that he is ill,' or, 'The fact that she is giving milk is a sign that she has lately borne a child.' Here we have the infallible kind of Sign."
> — Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, Bk I, Ch 2

### Prognosis

Hippocrates opens the Greek medical tradition of sign-reading with an argument for its practical necessity: since no physician can cure every patient, the highest skill available is to read present signs accurately enough to foretell the past, present, and future course of a disease — and it is this predictive accuracy, more than cure itself, that earns the patient's trust.

> "It appears to me a most excellent thing for the physician to cultivate Prognosis; for by foreseeing and foretelling, in the presence of the sick, the present, the past, and the future, and explaining the omissions which patients have been guilty of, he will be the more readily believed to be acquainted with the circumstances of the sick; so that men will have confidence to intrust themselves to such a physician."
> — Hippocrates, *Prognostics*, par. 1

### Prognostic Signs in Animals

Aristotle extends the logic of medical sign-reading past the human patient entirely: the same relation between visible surface and hidden internal state that lets a physician read a fever also lets an observer read a bird's ruffled feathers, showing that "symptom" names a general structure running through nature, not a device peculiar to human medicine.

> "Sickness in birds may be diagnosed from their plumage, which is ruffled when they are sickly instead of lying smooth as when they are well."
> — Aristotle, *History of Animals*, Bk VIII, Ch 18

### Reading Hidden Thought through Bodily Signs

Aquinas draws the outer limit of what any sign can disclose: a person's secret thoughts and the movements of their will are, strictly, knowable only to God directly, but their *effects* — a change of countenance, the beat of a pulse — leak outward into the body and become legible to any sufficiently attentive observer, human or angelic.

> "For thought is sometimes discovered not merely by outward act, but also by change of countenance; and doctors can tell some passions of the soul by the mere pulse."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 57, A 4

### The Satire of Over-Interpretation

Swift's Grand Academy of Lagado pushes the whole enterprise of reading hidden truths from external signs to its absurd limit, having statesmen "diagnose" treasonous plots from the color and texture of a suspect's excrement — a comic warning, sitting right alongside the section's serious medical semiotics, that inference from sign to hidden cause can shade into paranoid fabrication once the signifying relation is merely invented rather than observed.

> "He advised great statesmen to examine into the diet of all suspected persons... take a strict view of their excrements, and, from the colour, the odour, the taste, the consistence, the crudeness or maturity of digestion, form a judgment of their thoughts and designs; because men are never so serious, thoughtful, and intent, as when they are at stool."
> — Swift, *Gulliver's Travels*, Part III, Ch 6

### Sign (Semeion)

Aristotle's foundational definition, distinguishing the sign from the merely probable proposition: where a probability states what generally happens, a sign is a proposition — necessary or only generally true — from whose existence, or past occurrence, the existence or occurrence of some other thing can be inferred. This is the raw material of the enthymeme, rhetoric's counterpart to the syllogism.

> "A sign means a demonstrative proposition necessary or generally approved: for anything such that when it is another thing is, or when it has come into being the other has come into being before or after, is a sign of the other's being or having come into being."
> — Aristotle, *Prior Analytics*, Bk II, Ch 27

### Sign as Antecedent-Consequent

Hobbes strips the sign down to a purely empirical, non-logical relation: a sign is simply a past event that has reliably preceded (or followed) another, learned by repetition rather than demonstrated by necessity. On this account, reading signs correctly is not formal reasoning at all but experience accumulated into prudence — the more often a pairing has been observed, the more trustworthy the sign, but never with logical certainty.

> "A Signe, is the Event Antecedent, of the Consequent; and contrarily, the Consequent of the Antecedent, when the like Consequences have been observed, before: And the oftner they have been observed, the lesse uncertain is the Signe."
> — Hobbes, *Leviathan*, Part I, Ch 3

### The Unreliability of Symptoms

Fielding's comic surgeon voices, in a domestic register, the same caution embedded in Aristotle's fallible sign: a symptom's surface appearance is a poor guide to what lies beneath it, since a visibly severe injury (a fracture) can be less dangerous than an invisible one (a contusion), and even a single patient's symptoms can reverse themselves within a single day.

> "Symptoms," answered the surgeon, "are not always regular nor constant. I have known very unfavourable symptoms in the morning change to favourable ones at noon, and return to unfavourable again at night."
> — Fielding, *Tom Jones*, Bk VII, Ch 13

## 5a. Natural things as signs of divinity

*[Full section text](5a%20Natural%20things%20as%20signs%20of%20divinity/5a%20Natural%20things%20as%20signs%20of%20divinity.md)*

### Analogical Predication

The scholastic doctrine that words applied to God (*good*, *wise*, *living*) are neither used in exactly the same sense as when applied to creatures (univocally) nor in a wholly unrelated sense (equivocally), but *analogically* — creatures partially and imperfectly represent a perfection that exists in God without limit. This is the theoretical basis for how any created thing can function as a "sign" of God at all: it resembles its cause enough to point toward it, without being identical to it.

> "Therefore these names signify the divine substance, but in an imperfect manner, even as creatures represent it imperfectly."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 13, A 2

### Argument from Design

The inference from the observed order, regularity, and apparent purposefulness of the natural world to the existence of an intelligent governing cause — later called the teleological argument. Aquinas grounds it in ordinary experience: order implies an orderer, the way a tidy house implies someone tidied it.

> "If we enter a well ordered house we gather from it the intention of him who put it in order."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 103, A 1 (citing Aristotle via Cicero)

### Book of Nature

The idea, running through Bacon and much of this section, that creation itself is a second scripture alongside the written Word — one authored not in words but in things, to be read through observation rather than exegesis.

> "Let no man... think or maintain, that a man can search too far, or be too well studied in the book of God's word, or in the book of God's works, divinity or philosophy."
> — Bacon, *Advancement of Learning*

### Celestial Harmony

Kepler's conviction that the mathematical ratios governing planetary motion are not merely descriptive but doxological — that astronomy, done rightly, is a form of worship, and the order of the heavens is itself a hymn to their Maker.

> "I commence a sacred discourse, a most true hymn to God the Founder... I am free to give myself up to the sacred madness."
> — Kepler, *Harmonies of the World*

### Miracle

Distinguished in this section from ordinary providence: not simply anything wondrous, but a deliberate suspension of nature's own laws by their Author, for a specific and legible end — never performed, in Bacon's telling, merely to prove a point that reason could already establish on its own.

> "Thou never workest miracles, but to a divine and excellent end... the laws of nature are Thine own laws, and Thou exceedest them not but upon great cause."
> — Bacon, *New Atlantis*

### Natural Theology

Bacon's term for the branch of knowledge concerning God derived not from revelation but from reasoning about the created world — deliberately bounded: sufficient to refute atheism, but not sufficient to constitute faith.

> "Divine philosophy or natural theology... is that knowledge or rudiment of knowledge concerning God, which may be obtained by the contemplation of his creatures... The bounds of this knowledge are, that it sufficeth to convince atheism, but not to inform religion."
> — Bacon, *Advancement of Learning*

### Nature as Divine Language

Berkeley's immaterialist twist on the whole section's theme: sensible objects aren't signs pointing to some separate, hidden God behind them — they *are* God's speech, directly and continuously addressed to us, so that seeing anything at all is already an encounter with divinity.

> "We do at all times and in all places perceive manifest tokens of the Divinity: everything we see, hear, feel, or anywise perceive by sense, being a sign or effect of the power of God."
> — Berkeley, *Human Knowledge*

### Natural Variety as Testimony

The argument, here voiced in comic rather than theological register, that the sheer abundance and diversity of created things is itself evidence of divine wisdom and power — showing the idea reaching even into the novel, not just scripture and scholastic argument.

> "A variety which, as it must give great pleasure to a contemplative beholder, so doth it admirably display the power, and wisdom, and goodness of the Creator."
> — Fielding, *Tom Jones*

### Plenitude

Aquinas's answer to why creation is diverse rather than uniform: no single creature could adequately represent God's infinite goodness, so multiplicity itself — the sheer variety of things — is necessary to reflect a perfection no one thing could hold alone.

> "His goodness could not be adequately represented by one creature alone, He produced many and diverse creatures, that what was wanting to one in the representation of the divine goodness might be supplied by another."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 47, A 1

### Providence in Small Things

The Stoic argument that divine care is most convincingly visible not in grand cosmic events but in the fitness of the smallest, most easily overlooked details of the natural order.

> "Is there anything less useful than the hair on the chin? What then, has not nature used this hair also in the most suitable manner possible?"
> — Epictetus, *Discourses*, Bk I, Ch 16

### Sign (Signum)

The master concept of this whole Syntopicon chapter: something whose very existence or structure points beyond itself to something else. In this section specifically, a natural thing functions as a sign of the divine when its order or beauty is legible as evidence of an intelligent, invisible cause.

> "For the invisible things of him, from the creation of the world, are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made; his eternal power also, and divinity."
> — Romans 1:20

### Vestiges of the Trinity

Augustine's search for reflections of the divine Trinity not only in scripture but structurally, within the human mind itself — arguing that the triad of being, knowing, and loving in every person is a natural "footprint" of the Three Persons.

> "We are, and know that we are, and delight in our being, and our knowledge of it... as we run over all the works which He has established, we may detect, as it were, His footprints."
> — Augustine, *City of God*, Bk XI, Ch 26/28

### Wonder as Broken Knowledge

Bacon's careful distinction between two different outcomes of studying nature: contemplating creatures *as creatures* yields real, cumulative knowledge; contemplating them as a route *to God Himself* yields only wonder — genuine, but necessarily partial and unfinished.

> "The contemplation of God's creatures and works produceth (having regard to the works and creatures themselves) knowledge, but having regard to God, no perfect knowledge, but wonder which is broken knowledge."
> — Bacon, *Advancement of Learning*

## 5b. Supernatural signs, omens, portents, visitations, dreams, miracles

*[Full section text](5b%20Supernatural%20signs%2C%20omens%2C%20portents%2C%20visitations%2C%20dreams%2C%20miracles/5b%20Supernatural%20signs%2C%20omens%2C%20portents%2C%20visitations%2C%20dreams%2C%20miracles.md)*

### A Cause Absolutely Hidden

Aquinas's formal definition of *miracle*, built on the psychology of wonder rather than on any special class of event: a thing is marvelous when its cause is hidden from the observer, so the very same eclipse can be a miracle to a peasant and a predictable calculation to an astronomer. What makes an event a miracle in the strict sense is that its cause is hidden not just from one observer but from everyone — because the cause is God.

> "The word miracle is derived from admiration, which arises when an effect is manifest, and its cause is hidden... a thing is wonderful to one man, and not at all to others; as an eclipse is to a rustic, but not to an astronomer. Now a miracle is called so as being full of wonder, and as having a cause absolutely hidden from all; and this cause is God."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 105, A 7

### A Straw for All Dreams

Pandarus's dismissal of dream-interpretation runs through every rival theory the ancient and medieval world had on offer — divine revelation, demonic illusion, physiological complexion, the phase of the moon — only to conclude that no one actually knows what dreams mean. The irony Chaucer builds around this speech is the whole point: the skeptic is talking to the one man in the poem whose dream is about to come true.

> "A straw for alle swevenes signifiaunce! / God helpe me so, I counte hem not a bene, / Ther woot no man aright what dremes mene."
> — Chaucer, *Troilus and Cressida*, Bk V, Stanza 52

### After Our Own Fashion

Cicero's reply to Casca's breathless catalogue of Roman prodigies, delivered on the stormy night before Caesar's assassination: he does not deny that the portents are real, only that their meaning is self-evident. What a "strange-disposed time" is taken to signify depends less on the sky than on the fears and purposes of whoever is doing the reading.

> "Indeed, it is a strange-disposed time: / But men may construe things after their fashion, / Clean from the purpose of the things themselves."
> — Shakespeare, *Julius Caesar*, Act I, Sc iii

### Cause, Token, or Coincidence

Aristotle's alternative to the theory that prophetic dreams are sent by God: a dream that seems to come true is either a *cause* of the event, a *token* (symptom) of some bodily process already under way, or a mere *coincidence* — and since people have enormous numbers of dreams, some correspondences are statistically guaranteed without any supernatural transmission at all.

> "The dreams in question must be regarded either as causes, or as tokens, of the events, or else as coincidences... I use the word 'cause' in the sense in which the moon is [the cause] of an eclipse of the sun... 'token' [in the sense in which] the entrance of a star [into the shadow] is a token of the eclipse... while by 'coincidence' I mean, for example, the occurrence of an eclipse of the sun while some one is taking a walk."
> — Aristotle, *Prophesying*

### Death Is an Awakening

Prince Andrei's death comes to him first as a nightmare — a door that something monstrous is forcing open while he strains uselessly to hold it shut. Only at the instant the dream-death is completed does he wake into the realization that dying and waking are the same experience narrated from opposite sides of a threshold he has already crossed.

> "Yes, it was death! I died—and woke up. Yes, death is an awakening!"
> — Tolstoy, *War and Peace*, Bk XII

### Do Wise Men See Ghosts?

Plutarch's own hesitation, voiced in the middle of his narrative, about whether to credit the apparitions said to have warned both Dion and Brutus of their coming deaths. His argument for taking the visions seriously is not their strangeness but the character of the witnesses — solid, philosophical, unsuperstitious men not easily "deluded by fancy."

> "Yet if Dion and Brutus, men of solid understanding, and philosophers, not to be easily deluded by fancy or discomposed by any sudden apprehension, were thus affected by visions that they forthwith declared to their friends what they had seen, I know not how we can avoid admitting again the utterly exploded opinion of the oldest times, that evil and beguiling spirits... make efforts to excite in them feelings of terror and distraction..."
> — Plutarch, *Dion*

### Gates of Horn and Ivory

Penelope's explanation, to a stranger she does not yet know is her husband, of why she distrusts her own dream of the returning eagle: dreams travel to sleepers through two different gates, one of horn for the dreams that come true and one of ivory for the dreams that only deceive, and the dreamer has no way to tell which gate any particular dream has come through.

> "There are two gates through which these unsubstantial fancies proceed; the one is of horn, and the other ivory. Those that come through the gate of ivory are fatuous, but those from the gate of horn mean something to those that see them."
> — Homer, *Odyssey*, Bk XIX

### Gideon's Fleece

The origin of "putting out a fleece": unwilling to act on a spoken promise alone, Gideon demands a physical, repeatable sign from God, and then — having received it — deliberately asks for the same test run again with the results reversed, to rule out coincidence before he will commit his life to the divine commission.

> "I will put this fleece of wool on the floor: if there be dew on the fleece only, and it be dry on all the ground beside, I, shall know that by my hand, as thou hast said, thou wilt deliver Israel... And it was so."
> — Judges 6:37-38

### Mark of Cain

A sign that functions as curse and mercy in the same gesture: branding the first murderer permanently and visibly, yet instituted expressly to *protect* him from vengeance rather than invite it, so that the mark of maximum guilt becomes, paradoxically, a guarantee of safety.

> "Therefore whosoever slayeth Cain, vengeance shall be taken on him sevenfold. And the Lord set a mark upon Cain, lest any finding him should kill him."
> — Genesis 4:15

### Miracle, Mystery, and Authority

The Grand Inquisitor's account of why Christ's refusal to perform a demanded miracle — casting himself from the temple to prove his sonship — was the deepest of the three wilderness temptations he rejected. Humanity, the Inquisitor argues, cannot bear the freedom of an unproven faith, and will always trade that freedom to whatever authority is willing to supply certainty on demand.

> "There are three powers, three powers alone, able to conquer and to hold captive for ever the conscience of these impotent rebels for their happiness—those forces are miracle, mystery and authority."
> — Dostoevsky, *The Brothers Karamazov*, Bk V ("The Grand Inquisitor")

### Miracles and Doctrine

Pascal's statement of the circularity built into the whole apologetic use of miracles: a miracle is supposed to certify that a doctrine is true, yet it is doctrine that tells the faithful which miracles are genuinely of God and which are counterfeit. The rest of this section of the *Pensées* is Pascal working to escape that circle rather than deny it exists.

> "Miracles enable us to judge of doctrine, and doctrine enables us to judge of miracles."
> — Pascal, *Pensées*, 803

### The Balance of Experience

Hume's method for weighing any report of a miracle: since both the reliability of human testimony and the regularity of the laws of nature rest on the same foundation — past experience — a rational mind simply subtracts the weaker experience from the stronger and believes whichever way the remainder points. For a violation of nature's laws, he argues, the remainder can never favor belief.

> "It is experience only, which gives authority to human testimony; and it is the same experience, which assures us of the laws of nature. When, therefore, these two kinds of experience are contrary, we have nothing to do but substract the one from the other, and embrace an opinion, either on one side or the other, with that assurance which arises from the remainder."
> — Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, Sect X, Div 98

### The Daimonion

Socrates's private "divine sign," a voice he says had guided him since childhood — but only ever by forbidding, never by commanding. Its most significant appearance in the *Apology* is an absence: its total silence on the morning of his trial and death is what persuades him the verdict, though fatal, must actually be good.

> "But the oracle made no sign of opposition, either when I was leaving my house in the morning, or when I was on my way to the court, or while I was speaking, at anything which I was going to say... It is an intimation that what has happened to me is a good, and that those of us who think that death is an evil are in error."
> — Plato, *Apology*

### The Darkness Nobody Noticed

Gibbon's driest and most devastating argument against the reliability of the Gospel miracles: Pliny and Seneca each compiled exhaustive catalogues of every unusual darkening of the sun recorded in their lifetimes, yet neither so much as mentions the three-hour darkness said to have covered the earth at the Crucifixion — a phenomenon that, if real, should have been the single most remarkable thing either of them had ever recorded.

> "The sages of Greece and Rome turned aside from the awful spectacle, and, pursuing the ordinary occupations of life and study, appeared unconscious of any alterations in the moral or physical government of the world... Both the one and the other have omitted to mention the greatest phenomenon to which the mortal eye has been witness since the creation of the globe."
> — Gibbon, *Decline and Fall*

### The Historian and the Prodigy

Tacitus's stated policy for handling the omens and prodigies that turn up throughout Roman tradition: he will neither endorse them as literal fact nor suppress them as beneath a serious historian's dignity, since recording what people believed happened is itself part of the historian's task, whatever he privately makes of it.

> "I should think it unbecoming the dignity of the task which I have undertaken, to collect fabulous marvels, and to amuse with fiction the tastes of my readers; at the same time I would not venture to impugn the credit of common report and tradition."
> — Tacitus, *Histories*, Bk II

### The Meaningful Slip

Freud's modern, secularized descendant of the ancient omen: forgetting is never simply mechanical. An unwelcome name or memory is not lost at random but is actively pushed out of awareness by an unconscious motive, so that what looks like an accidental lapse is, on analysis, a disguised piece of self-revelation rather than a meaningless gap.

> "That unwelcome impressions are easily forgotten is an indubitable fact. Various psychologists have remarked it; and the great Darwin was so well aware of it that he made a golden rule for himself of writing down with particular care observations which seemed unfavourable to his theory, having become convinced that just these would be inclined to slip out of recollection."
> — Freud, *A General Introduction to Psycho-Analysis*

### The Portent at Aulis

The omen witnessed by the Greek fleet before it ever reaches Troy: two eagles devouring a pregnant hare. Calchas reads it as a single sign carrying two incompatible meanings at once — triumph for the kings it favors, and the goddess's fury at the manner of that triumph — so that the portent forecasts victory and grief in the very same breath.

> "A hare their meat, all quick with young, / Ta'en, her last doublings o'er. / Be Sorrow, Sorrow's burden sung. / But crown Joy conqueror!"
> — Aeschylus, *Agamemnon*

### The Siren Unmasked

Dante's dream-vision of a stammering, deformed woman who turns beautiful the instant the dreamer's own gaze settles on her and starts to sing of the pleasure she gives — until a second figure tears her robe open and exposes the rot underneath. The allegory makes explicit what the whole section elsewhere leaves implicit: a tempting vision draws its power from the dreamer's own desire, not from anything true in the vision itself.

> "She took hold of the other, and in front she opened her, rending her garments, and showed me her belly; this waked me with the stench that issued from it."
> — Dante, *Divine Comedy*, Purgatory, Canto XIX

### This Beginning of Miracles

John's own label for the water turned to wine at Cana, the first in a deliberate sequence he calls "signs" throughout his Gospel: not simply displays of power for their own sake, but events with a stated purpose — to manifest Christ's glory and produce belief in the people who witness them.

> "This beginning of miracles did Jesus in Cana of Galilee; and manifested his glory, and his disciples believed in him."
> — John 2:11

### Vision or Dream?

Hobbes's clinical dismantling of supernatural apparitions, illustrated with Brutus's famous ghost on the eve of Philippi: a man who is anxious, cold, and asleep without realizing he has fallen asleep cannot distinguish his own dream from a genuine waking vision — and history books, Hobbes argues, have been recording the resulting confusion as literal supernatural visitation ever since.

> "We read of Marcus Brutus... how at Philippi, the night before he gave battle to Augustus Caesar, he saw a fearful apparition, which is commonly related by historians as a vision, but, considering the circumstances, one may easily judge to have been but a short dream... having no assurance that he slept, he could have no cause to think it a dream, or anything but a vision."
> — Hobbes, *Leviathan*, Part I

### Why Miracles Ceased

Augustine's answer to the standing objection that miracles no longer occur as they did in apostolic times: miracles were the scaffolding needed to build the world's belief in the first place, and once that belief is established the scaffolding is no longer required. The demand to see fresh miracles now, he adds pointedly, is itself a symptom of unbelief.

> "Why, they say, are those miracles, which you affirm were wrought formerly, wrought no longer?... Whoever nowadays demands to see prodigies that he may believe, is himself a great prodigy, because he does not believe, though the whole world does."
> — Augustine, *City of God*, Bk XXII, Ch 8

## 5c. The symbolism of the sacraments and of sacramental or ritualistic acts

*[Full section text](5c%20The%20symbolism%20of%20the%20sacraments%20and%20of%20sacramental%20or%20ritualistic%20acts/5c.%20The%20symbolism%20of%20the%20sacraments%20and%20of%20sacramental%20or%20ritualistic%20acts.md)*

### Bondage to Signs

Augustine's rule for telling superstition from true religion: whoever honors a sacred sign without understanding what it points to is enslaved to the sign itself, while whoever understands its reference honors not the sign but the reality behind it. On this basis he judges the ceremonial law of the Old Testament a "useful" bondage — restrictive, but still bondage to true signs — sharply unlike the Gentiles' bondage to signs of nothing at all.

> "Now he is in bondage to a sign who uses, or pays homage to, any significant object without knowing what it signifies: he, on the other hand, who either uses or honours a useful sign divinely appointed, whose force and significance he understands, does not honour the sign which is seen and temporal, but that to which all such signs refer."
> — Augustine, *Christian Doctrine*, Bk III, Ch 9

*See also:* **[Bondage to Signs](../3c%20%281%29%20The%20same%20word%20used%20literally%20and%20figuratively-%20metaphors/3c1%20Glossary.md#bondage-to-signs)** in *3c(1). The same word used literally and figuratively: metaphors* — the general principle Augustine states two chapters earlier in the same book: that fixation on the sign itself, never rising to what it signifies, is "a miserable slavery of the soul." This entry is the specific case built on it — the Old Law's ceremonial signs as a *useful* instance of that same bondage.

### Commemoration

The Gospel and Pauline accounts of the Last Supper establish the Eucharist as a ritual act of remembrance instituted by direct command. Bread and wine are identified with Christ's body and blood, and the disciples are explicitly charged to repeat the act "for the commemoration" of him, turning a single historical meal into a perpetually reiterated sign.

> "Take ye, and eat: this is my body, which shall be delivered for you: this do for the commemoration of me."
> — 1 Corinthians 11:24

### Instrumental Causality

Aquinas's answer to how a physical rite can do something as immaterial as confer grace: not as a principal cause (that power belongs to God alone) but as an instrumental cause, the way a craftsman's axe is moved by his art to shape a couch it does not resemble. Because the sacraments are moved by divine power toward their effect, they are said to bring about what they signify rather than merely represent it.

> "...the sacraments of the New Law cause grace, for they are instituted by God to be employed for the purpose of conferring grace... to use the common expression, they effect what they signify."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part III, Q 62, A 1

### Oath of Allegiance

Hobbes redefines the sacraments in political rather than mystical terms: a sacrament is simply a visible thing set apart from ordinary use to mark either admission into a commonwealth (circumcision, baptism) or commemorative renewal of loyalty to it (Passover, the Lord's Supper). Holiness, on this account, is a public, civil signal of allegiance rather than a channel of hidden grace.

> "A sacrament is a separation of some visible thing from common use; and a consecration of it to God's service, for a sign either of our admission into the kingdom of God, to be of the number of his peculiar people, or for a commemoration of the same."
> — Hobbes, *Leviathan*, Part III

### Sacrifice as Sign

Augustine's account of why the Old Testament commanded animal sacrifices God did not actually want: no external sacrifice was ever valued for itself, only as the visible token of an internal one — the offering of a contrite heart — so that the entire apparatus of Levitical sacrifice is, properly understood, a sign system rather than a transaction with a needy deity.

> "A sacrifice, therefore, is the visible sacrament or sacred sign of an invisible sacrifice."
> — Augustine, *City of God*, Bk X, Ch 5

### Sensible Signs

Aquinas's explanation for why the sacraments must be physical acts involving physical matter — water, oil, bread — rather than purely mental or verbal ones: since all human knowledge begins in the senses, a wholly spiritual sign could never reach a mind that must first pass through the sensible world to grasp anything intelligible at all.

> "Now it is natural to man to acquire knowledge of the intelligible from the sensible. But a sign is that by means of which one attains to the knowledge of something else... it follows that the sacramental signs consist in sensible things, just as in the Divine Scriptures spiritual things are set before us under the likeness of things sensible."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part III, Q 60, A 4

### Sensible Types

Hume's naturalistic explanation of why Catholic ceremony works psychologically, without granting it any supernatural warrant: because resemblance between an image and its object "enlivens" an idea the way a present impression does, worshippers who handle sensible types and images of sacred things feel their devotion strengthened by simple association — exactly as a portrait intensifies feeling for an absent friend.

> "The ceremonies of the Roman Catholic religion may be considered as instances of the same nature... We shadow out the objects of our faith, say they, in sensible types and images, and render them more present to us by the immediate presence of these types, than it is possible for us to do merely by an intellectual view and contemplation."
> — Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, Sect V, Div 41

### Shadow and Image

Aquinas's account of why the Old Law's ceremonies had to be figurative: since human beings in this life cannot look directly on divine truth, worship must come wrapped in sensible figures. Because Christ had not yet come, the Old Law's figures could only be a "shadow" of what was still future, in contrast to the New Law's fuller, nearer "image" of the same reality.

> "The Law has... a shadow of the good things to come, not the very image of the things, for a shadow is less than an image, so that the image belongs to the New Law, but the shadow to the Old."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I-II, Q 101, A 2

### Sign of the Covenant

The Genesis account establishes circumcision not as a hygienic or tribal custom but explicitly as a sign — a mark in the flesh whose entire purpose is to be legible, both to God and to Abraham's descendants, as physical proof of a specific promise. The same formula, something commanded to function "for a sign," recurs throughout the Pentateuch for the Sabbath, the Passover blood, and the phylacteries bound on hand and forehead.

> "And you shall circumcise the flesh of your foreskin, that it may be for a sign of the covenant between me and you."
> — Genesis 17:11

### Sign of Washing

In Michael's prophecy to Adam near the end of *Paradise Lost*, baptism is introduced not through doctrine but through epic narration of future history: the rite the apostles will one day perform is compressed into a single clean phrase identifying the physical washing explicitly as a "signe" of an inward, moral cleansing from sin.

> "Baptizing in the profluent streame, the signe / Of washing them from guilt of sin to Life / Pure, and in mind prepar'd, if so befall,"
> — Milton, *Paradise Lost*, Bk XII [436-445]

### The Externality of the Holy

Hegel's diagnosis of what went wrong with medieval sacramental religion: by locating the sacred in a "mere thing" — the consecrated Host, adored even apart from being received in communion — the Church let holiness become an external object that could be possessed by some and withheld from others, splitting Christendom into a priesthood that dispenses grace and a laity that can only passively receive it.

> "The Holy as a mere thing has the character of externality; thus it is capable of being taken possession of by another to my exclusion: it may come into an alien hand, since the process of appropriating it is not one that takes place in spirit, but is conditioned by its quality as an external object."
> — Hegel, *Philosophy of History*, Part IV

### The Hieroglyph

In the elaborate blindfolded ritual of Pierre's induction into the Freemasons, his initiator explains the whole method of the ceremony — skull, coffin, sword, darkness — by way of a single piece of sign-theory: the Order teaches through hieroglyphs, objects that stand for realities the senses cannot directly grasp precisely because they share some quality of resemblance with what they represent.

> "A hieroglyph," said the Rhetor, "is an emblem of something not cognizable by the senses but which possesses qualities resembling those of the symbol."
> — Tolstoy, *War and Peace*, Bk V, Ch 3

### The Labarum

Gibbon's ironic history of the cross's transformation from a Roman instrument of degrading execution into the most potent political-military symbol of the empire: once adopted by Constantine, the same shape that had signified "guilt, pain, and ignominy" was refashioned as an invincible battle standard, its meaning entirely reversed by imperial will rather than by anything intrinsic to the sign itself.

> "There is still extant a medal of the emperor Constantius, where the standard of the labarum is accompanied with these memorable words, By this sign thou shalt conquer."
> — Gibbon, *Decline and Fall*

### Tree of Penitence

The Parson organizes his entire treatise on penance around a single controlling image: contrition is the hidden root, confession the visible trunk and branches, and satisfaction the fruit — so that the three canonically required parts of the sacrament of Penance are not arbitrary steps but organically dependent stages of one living process.

> "The root of this tree is contrition, which hides itself away in the heart of him who is truly repentant, just as the root of another tree hides within the earth. From the root contrition springs a trunk that bears branches and leaves of confession and the fruit of penance."
> — Chaucer, *Parson's Tale*, par 6

### Type and Presence

Pascal's diagnosis of how heresies about the Eucharist typically arise: not by inventing something false outright, but by seizing on one true half of the Catholic doctrine — that the sacrament is a commemorative type of Christ's sacrifice — and using it to deny the other true half, that Christ is also really present, when orthodoxy holds that a single sacrament can be both at once without contradiction.

> "...this Sacrament is also a type of the cross and of glory, and a commemoration of the two. That is the Catholic faith, which comprehends these two truths which seem opposed."
> — Pascal, *Pensées*, 862

## 5d. The symbolism of numbers in theology

*[Full section text](5d%20The%20symbolism%20of%20numbers%20in%20theology/5d.%20The%20symbolism%20of%20numbers%20in%20theology.md)*

### Comedy of Sevens

Sterne turns the same number-mysticism that fills the rest of this section into farce. In the middle of a mock-theological squabble between Uncle Toby and the Catholic Dr. Slop, "seven" becomes a stand-in for the entire apparatus of doctrinal enumeration — sacraments, virtues, sins, heavens — rattled off so promiscuously that the number's supposed sacredness collapses into a punchline.

> "Why, Sir, are there not seven cardinal virtues?—Seven mortal sins?—Seven golden candlesticks?—Seven heavens?... Are there not seven wonders of the world? Seven days of the creation? Seven planets? Seven plagues?"
> — Sterne, *Tristram Shandy*

### Cubic New Jerusalem

John's vision of the heavenly city is measured out entirely in multiples of twelve — twelve gates, twelve foundations, twelve thousand furlongs to a side — and its most striking feature is geometric rather than narrative: the city is a perfect cube, its length, breadth, and height all equal, a shape ancient readers took as the ultimate figure of completeness and stability.

> "And the city lieth in a foursquare, and the length thereof is as great as the breadth: and he measured the city with the golden reed for twelve thousand furlongs, and the length and the height and the breadth thereof are equal."
> — Revelation 21:16

### Gematria of 666

Awaiting Napoleon's downfall, Tolstoy's Pierre Bezukhov applies a Hebrew-style numeric cipher to the French alphabet and finds, to his excitement, that "L'Empereur Napoleon" sums to 666. When his own name doesn't quite work out, he simply adjusts the spelling until it does — Tolstoy quietly showing how confirmation bias, not revelation, drives this kind of number-hunting.

> "Then it occurred to him: if the answer to the question were contained in his name, his nationality would also be given in the answer. So he wrote Le russe Besuhof and adding up the numbers got 671. This was only five too much... By omitting the e, though incorrectly, Pierre got the answer he sought. L'russe Besuhof made 666."
> — Tolstoy, *War and Peace*, Bk IX

### Hundredfold Fruit

Explaining why continence is rewarded in Scripture with three different multiples — thirtyfold for marriage, sixtyfold for widowhood, a hundredfold for virginity — Aquinas treats the numbers themselves as doctrinally meaningful rather than arbitrary: 30 is 3 (the number of any complete thing's beginning, middle, and end) times 10 (the Commandments), while 100, the "limit of numbers," fittingly caps virginity, "the limit of spirituality."

> "For 30 is the product of 3 multiplied by 10. Now 3 is the number of everything... and contains a certain perfection common to all, namely of beginning, middle, and end... The number 100 corresponds fittingly to virginity, because the number 10 of which 100 is a multiple is the limit of numbers, and in like manner virginity occupies the limit of spirituality, since no further spirituality can be added to it."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part III Suppl, Q 96, A 4

### Legitimate Numbers

Expounding Tichonius's rules for interpreting Scripture, Augustine explains why certain numbers — seven, ten, twelve, and their products — recur so insistently in the biblical text: Scripture "favours" them as shorthand for totality, so that a number multiplied by itself, like twelve by twelve, can stand not for an actual head-count but for the whole body of the redeemed.

> "our author calls those numbers legitimate which Holy Scripture more highly favours, such as seven, or ten, or twelve... Now numbers of this sort are often put for time universal... or when multiplied into themselves, as ten into ten gives one hundred, and twelve into twelve gives one hundred and forty-four, which last number is used in the Apocalypse to signify the whole body of the saints."
> — Augustine, *Christian Doctrine*, Bk III, Ch 35

### Measure, Number, and Weight

Answering whether goodness itself consists in "mode, species, and order," Aquinas builds his account of what it means for a created thing to be good around a single verse from Wisdom: number gives a thing its determinate kind, measure gives it its proper limit, and weight gives it the inclination that carries it toward its end — making arithmetic one of the structural categories of creation itself.

> "Thou hast ordered all things in measure, and number, and weight (Wisd 11. 21)... 'Measure marks the mode of everything, number gives it its species, and weight gives it rest and stability.'"
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 5, A 5, Obj 1 (quoting Wisdom 11:21 and Augustine)

### Number of the Beast

The Apocalypse ends its portrait of the Antichrist's agent not with a name but with a cipher, daring the reader to decode it: a specific number, tied explicitly to "a man," that has invited nearly two thousand years of numerological identification, from first-century Roman emperors to Tolstoy's Napoleon.

> "Here is wisdom. He that hath understanding, let him count the number of the beast. For it is the number of a man: and the number of him is six hundred sixty-six."
> — Revelation 13:18

### Odd Numbers for the Celestial Gods

Among the many Pythagorean-style precepts Plutarch attributes to Numa, Rome's second king, is a rule of ritual arithmetic: the class of deity being addressed determines the class of number used in sacrifice, gods above answered with odd numbers, gods of the earth with even — a distinction Plutarch admits even the Pythagoreans themselves would not commonly explain.

> "When thou sacrificest to the celestial gods, let it be with an odd number, and when to the terrestrial, with even."
> — Plutarch, *Numa Pompilius*

### Perfect Number Six

Augustine argues that God did not need six days to create the world — He could have made everything instantly — but chose that span because six is mathematically "perfect," the first number exactly equal to the sum of its own divisors (1+2+3=6). Creation's timetable, on this reading, is itself a piece of number-theology: the world's completeness in six days mirrors the arithmetical completeness of the number itself.

> "These works are recorded to have been completed in six days... because six is a perfect number—not because God required a protracted time... but because the perfection of the works was signified by the number six. For the number six is the first which is made up of its own parts, i.e., of its sixth, third, and half, which are respectively one, two, and three, and which make a total of six."
> — Augustine, *City of God*, Bk XI, Ch 30

### Sabbath and the Seven Days

The fourth commandment grounds the weekly day of rest not in convenience or custom but directly in the structure of creation itself: because God worked six days and rested the seventh, the number seven becomes sanctified, binding cosmology to calendar and giving the week — an arbitrary human unit almost everywhere else — a theological necessity.

> "For in six days the Lord made heaven and earth, and the sea, and all things that are in them, and rested on the seventh day: therefore the Lord blessed the seventh day, and sanctified it."
> — Exodus 20:11

### Sealing of the 144,000

Before the great tribulation breaks, an angel halts the four winds of destruction long enough for God's servants to be sealed — a headcount built from pure multiplication rather than a narrative census: twelve thousand from each of the twelve tribes of Israel, yielding a total whose completeness lies in its arithmetic as much as its size.

> "And I heard the number of them that were signed, an hundred forty-four thousand were signed, of every tribe of the children of Israel."
> — Revelation 7:4

### Trinity in the Sphere

Kepler reads the geometry of the cosmos itself as a diagram of Christian doctrine: because a sphere is "the image of God the Creator," its three structural parts — center, surface, and the space between — stand for the three Persons of the Trinity, with the sun, the fixed stars, and the planetary region occupying that same threefold pattern.

> "in the sphere, which is the image of God the Creator and the Archetype of the world... there are three regions, symbols of the three persons of the Holy Trinity—the centre, a symbol of the Father; the surface, of the Son; and the intermediate space, of the Holy Ghost."
> — Kepler, *Epitome*, Bk IV

## 5e. The interpretation of the word of God

*[Full section text](5e%20The%20interpretation%20of%20the%20word%20of%20God/5e.%20The%20interpretation%20of%20the%20word%20of%20God.md)*

### The Ambiguous Oracle

Herodotus's diagnosis of why the Delphic oracle told Croesus the truth and still ruined him: its prophecy that he would "destroy a mighty empire" was genuinely double-edged, and Croesus simply assumed the flattering reading without asking which empire was meant. The Pythia's own verdict afterward puts the burden of disambiguation squarely on the interpreter, not on the oracle.

> "For when the god told him that, if he attacked the Persians, he would destroy a mighty empire, he ought, if he had been wise, to have sent again and inquired which empire was meant, that of Cyrus or his own; but if he neither understood what was said, nor took the trouble to seek for enlightenment, he has only himself to blame for the result."
> — Herodotus, *History*, Bk I [91]

*See also:* **[The Ambiguous Oracle](../3a%20Verbal%20ambiguity%20indefiniteness%20or%20multiplicity%20of%20meaning/3a%20Glossary.md#the-ambiguous-oracle)** in *3a. Verbal ambiguity: indefiniteness or multiplicity of meaning* — the same Croesus story, read there as the paradigm case of oracular language exploiting multiplicity of meaning, with the companion riddle (the "mule" who turns out to be Cyrus) this entry leaves out.

### The Beast Numbered

Pierre Bezukhov's private exercise in gematria — mapping letters onto numbers until "L'Empereur Napoleon" sums to 666 — is Tolstoy's ironic, secular echo of apocalyptic interpretation: a method borrowed wholesale from scriptural numerology, applied by a half-convinced amateur to current events, and quietly adjusted until it yields the answer he was already looking for.

> "Writing the words L'Empereur Napoleon in numbers, it appears that the sum of them is 666, and that Napoleon was therefore the beast foretold in the Apocalypse."
> — Tolstoy, *War and Peace*, Bk IX

### Divine Condescension

Beatrice's explanation, in Dante, for why Scripture and the Church describe God and the angels in bodily terms — hands, feet, human faces — even though the blessed have no true shape or place at all: revelation deliberately stoops to what the senses can hold, because the intellect can rise to higher things only by first passing through them.

> "For this the Scripture condescends to your capacity, and attributes feet and hands to God, and means otherwise; and Holy Church represents to you Gabriel and Michael with human aspect, and the other who made Tobias whole again."
> — Dante, *Divine Comedy*, Paradise, Canto IV

### The Four Senses of Scripture

Aquinas's answer to how a single scriptural passage can mean more than any human author intends: because God signifies not only through words but through the very things the words describe, a text carries a literal sense plus three further senses built on it — the allegorical (the Old Law read as a figure of the New), the moral (both read as a pattern for present conduct), and the anagogical (read as a figure of future glory).

> "So far as the things of the Old Law signify the things of the New Law, there is the allegorical sense. But so far as the things done in Christ, or so far as the things which signify Christ, are types of what we ought to do, there is the moral sense. But so far as they signify what relates to eternal glory, there is the anagogical sense."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 1, A 10

### The Grand Inquisitor's Reinterpretation

Ivan's fable turns Christ's three temptations in the wilderness into a text the Inquisitor claims to understand better than Christ understood His own refusal of them, reading the offer of bread, miracle, and authority not as things rightly rejected but as the very instruments the Church would always need — an interpretation that inverts its source while insisting it only completes it.

> "Judge Thyself who was right—Thou or he who questioned Thee then? Remember the first question; its meaning, in other words, was this: 'Thou wouldst go into the world, and art going with empty hands, with some promise of freedom which men in their simplicity and their natural unruliness cannot even understand, which they fear and dread—for nothing has ever been more insupportable for a man and a human society than freedom.'"
> — Dostoevsky, *The Brothers Karamazov*, Bk V (The Grand Inquisitor)

### Interpretation by Tone of Voice

A comic demonstration, staged during Corporal Trim's reading of a sermon on Hebrews 13:18, that interpretation often happens before any argument begins. Walter Shandy objects that Trim's sneering delivery has already smuggled in a reading of "Trust! Trust we have a good conscience!" — that the preacher means to mock the Apostle — which the bare words themselves don't require.

> "Certainly, Trim, quoth my father, interrupting him, you give that sentence a very improper accent; for you curl up your nose, man, and read it with such a sneering tone, as if the Parson was going to abuse the Apostle.
> He is, an' please your Honour, replied Trim."
> — Sterne, *Tristram Shandy*

### The Marks of a True Prophet

Hobbes's civil test for telling a genuine revelation from an impostor's claim to one, offered as the ground rule for all scriptural authority in a Christian commonwealth: neither a miracle alone nor sound doctrine alone will do, since a wonder-worker preaching revolt is no more to be trusted than a preacher of orthodoxy who performs no sign — only the two together mark a true prophet.

> "There be two marks by which together, not asunder, a true prophet is to be known. One is the doing of miracles; the other is the not teaching any other religion than that which is already established."
> — Hobbes, *Leviathan*, Part III, Ch 32

### The Mazes of Allegory

Gibbon's account of how the Paulician heretics evaded correction on relics, images, and the sacraments: whenever the literal sense of Scripture stood against them, they simply retreated into figurative reading — using allegory not to uncover a text's meaning but to make their own position unfalsifiable.

> "They indulged a convenient latitude for the interpretation of Scripture: and as often as they were pressed by the literal sense, they could escape to the intricate mazes of figure and allegory."
> — Gibbon, *Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire*

### No Private Interpretation

Peter's warning that prophecy is not a text open to any individual reader's construction: because it did not originate in "the will of man" but in men moved by the Holy Ghost, its meaning cannot be settled by private judgment either — a claim that becomes the flashpoint for nearly every later argument in this section over who holds the authority to interpret.

> "Understanding this first, that no prophecy of scripture is made by private interpretation. For prophecy came not by the will of man at any time: but the holy men of God spoke, inspired by the Holy Ghost."
> — II Peter 1:20-21

### The Oracle-Monger

A stock figure across several of Aristophanes's comedies: the professional reader of Bakis's verses who discovers in a deliberately vague oracle whatever meaning serves his own profit, then reverses the reading the instant it stops being convenient. The joke — that an obscure enough text can be made to mean almost anything, even that a place-name doesn't really mean that place — depends on real anxieties about oracular and scriptural interpretation alike.

> "O.-M. Nay but if once grey crows and wolves shall be banding together, Out in the midway space, 'twixt Corinth and Sicyon, dwelling—
> Pe. But what in the world have I to do with Corinth?
> O.-M. Bakis is riddling: Bakis means the Air."
> — Aristophanes, *Birds* [959-991]

### The Plurality of True Senses

Augustine's resolution of his own agonizing over what Moses "meant" by a given phrase of Genesis: unable to be certain which of several true readings the author intended, he concludes that an author writing with God's own generosity would deliberately choose words capacious enough to hold every true meaning a faithful reader might find in them, rather than one meaning narrow enough to exclude the rest.

> "For I certainly (and fearlessly I speak it from my heart) that were I to indite anything to have supreme authority, I should prefer so to write that whatever truth any could apprehend on those matters might be conveyed in my words, rather than set down my own meaning so clearly as to exclude the rest, which, not being false, could not offend me."
> — Augustine, *Confessions*, Bk XII, par 42

### Reconciling Scripture with Reason

Aquinas's working rule whenever a literal reading of Genesis brushes against natural philosophy: hold Scripture's truth without wavering, but hold any particular interpretation of it loosely enough to abandon if it is ever shown false — since defending a literal reading that is demonstrably wrong only exposes the faith to ridicule and blocks unbelievers from the rest of it.

> "In discussing questions of this kind two rules are to be observed, as Augustine teaches... The first is, to hold the truth of Scripture without wavering. The second is that since Holy Scripture can be explained in a multiplicity of senses, one should adhere to a particular explanation only in such measure as to be ready to abandon it if it be proved with certainty to be false, lest Holy Scripture be exposed to the ridicule of unbelievers, and obstacles be placed to their believing."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 68, A 1

### Suspended Judgment on the Oracle

The Chorus's response, in Sophocles, to the Delphic pronouncement implicating Oedipus: not disbelief in the god's word, but a refusal to accept any human being's application of it to a particular case until events themselves confirm the reading. It draws a line between an oracle's own infallibility and the fallibility of whoever claims to have correctly construed it.

> "Nay, Zeus indeed and Apollo are keen of thought, and know the things of earth; but that mortal seer wins knowledge above mine, of this there can be no sure test; though man may surpass man in lore. Yet, until I see the word made good, never will I assent when men blame Oedipus."
> — Sophocles, *Oedipus the King* [463-512]

### The Two Books

Bacon's argument that Scripture and the created world are companion volumes from the same author: the study of nature is not theology's rival but "a key" to it, guarding believers against both credulous error and against reading Scripture's occasional scientific-sounding language too literally.

> "For our Saviour saith, 'You err, not knowing the Scriptures, nor the power of God'; laying before us two books or volumes to study, if we will be secured from error; first the Scriptures, revealing the will of God, and then the creatures expressing his power; whereof the latter is a key unto the former."
> — Bacon, *Advancement of Learning*

### The Two Testaments and the Double Meaning

Pascal's compressed statement of the typological case for Christianity: the only way to settle whether Jesus was the promised Messiah is to ask whether Old Testament prophecy carries one sense or two, since on a single literal reading the Messiah plainly has not come, while on a doubled reading — carnal promise fulfilled in Israel, spiritual promise fulfilled in Christ — the prophecies are satisfied exactly.

> "To prove the two at one stroke, we need only see if the prophecies in one are fulfilled in the other. To examine the prophecies, we must understand them. For if we believe they have only one meaning, it is certain that the Messiah has not come; but if they have two meanings, it is certain that He has come in Jesus Christ. The whole problem then is to know if they have two meanings."
> — Pascal, *Pensées*, 642

### The Writing on the Wall

The Old Testament's most literal image of "the interpretation of the word": Belshazzar's whole court is struck helpless by a message it cannot even read, and Daniel's gift is not sharper eyesight but the God-given ability to supply the meaning the bare letters withhold — decipherment and prophecy collapsed into a single act.

> "And this is the writing that is written: MANE, THECEL, PHARES. And this is the interpretation of the word. MANE: God hath numbered thy kingdom, and hath finished it. THECEL: thou art weighed in the balance, and art found wanting. PHARES: thy kingdom is divided, and is given to the Medes and Persians."
> — Daniel 5:25-28

## 5f. The names of God: the use of words to signify the divine nature

*[Full section text](5f%20The%20names%20of%20God%20the%20use%20of%20words%20to%20signify%20the%20divine%20nature/5f.%20The%20names%20of%20God%20the%20use%20of%20words%20to%20signify%20the%20divine%20nature.md)*

### Borrowed Divine Names

Herodotus's deflationary account of how the Greeks came to have names for their gods at all: imported wholesale from Egypt, then merely ratified — not originated — by the oracle at Dodona, which was asked only whether to adopt the foreign names, not what the gods' true names were. It treats divine naming as a matter of cultural transmission and institutional approval rather than revelation.

> "Not long after the arrival of the names they sent to consult the oracle at Dodona about them. This is the most ancient oracle in Greece, and at that time there was no other. To their question, 'Whether they should adopt the names that had been imported from the foreigners?' the oracle replied by recommending their use."
> — Herodotus, *History*, Bk II [60a-d]

### Divine Ineffability

Augustine's paradox: to call God "unspeakable" is already to have spoken of Him, so the very word meant to mark the limit of language quietly breaks it. He concludes that silence, not further speech, is the truer response to the contradiction — even as he goes on, of necessity, speaking anyway.

> "Have I spoken of God, or uttered His praise, in any worthy way? Nay, I feel that I have done nothing more than desire to speak; and if I have said anything, it is not what I desired to say... And so God is not even to be called 'unspeakable,' because to say even this is to speak of Him."
> — Augustine, *Christian Doctrine*, Bk I, Ch 6

### God as a Relative Name

Newton's careful distinction, in the General Scholium, between God's absolute attributes — eternal, infinite, perfect — and the name "God" itself, which he argues signifies something else entirely: lordship, a relation to servants. A being however perfect, he insists, is not thereby a "God" unless it also has dominion; the word names a relationship, not merely a nature.

> "...for God is a relative word, and has a respect to servants... The word God usually signifies Lord; but every lord is not a God. It is the dominion of a spiritual being which constitutes a God."
> — Newton, *Principles*, Bk III, General Scholium

### He Who Is

Aquinas's argument, drawn straight from Exodus, that "He Who Is" is the single most proper name available for God, because unlike every other name it signifies no determinate form or mode of being, only being itself — "an infinite and indeterminate sea of substance," as Damascene put it, which Aquinas approvingly cites.

> "This name, he who is, is most properly applied to God, for three reasons. First, because of its signification. For it does not signify form, but being itself."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 13, A 11

### I Am Who Am

The divine self-naming at the burning bush, where God answers Moses's request for a name not with a proper name among other names but with a declaration of pure existence itself. This passage anchors the whole section: nearly every later writer who asks what it means to name God returns to it, above all Aquinas, who argues centuries later that it is the most proper name precisely because it signifies being itself rather than any particular nature.

> "God said to Moses: I AM WHO AM. He said: Thus shalt thou say to the children of Israel: HE WHO IS, hath sent me to you."
> — Exodus, 3:13-14

### Naming as Honor, Not Comprehension

Hobbes's deflationary account of why we call God anything at all: not because any name captures His nature — He is by definition incomprehensible — but because refusing to name Him, or naming Him carelessly, would fail to show Him the honor due a sovereign beyond our understanding.

> "And therefore the name of God is used, not to make us conceive Him (for He is incomprehensible, and His greatness and power are unconceivable), but that we may honour Him."
> — Hobbes, *Leviathan*, Part I

### Reverent Agnosticism About the Gods' Names

Socrates' sudden check on his own etymologizing in the *Cratylus*: whatever ingenious derivations mortals propose, the gods' own names for themselves are unknown to us, and only their self-given names — not our guesses — can be called true. It is a rare moment of principled humility inside a dialogue otherwise devoted to confident, playful name-derivation.

> "Yes, indeed, Hermogenes; and there is one excellent principle which, as men of sense, we must acknowledge,—that of the Gods we know nothing, either of their natures or of the names which they give themselves; but we are sure that the names by which they call themselves, whatever they may be, are true."
> — Plato, *Cratylus*, 93d-97d

### The Analogy of Names

Aquinas's solution to how any word could apply to both God and creatures: neither univocally, in exactly the same sense (which would make God just another instance of a kind), nor equivocally, in a wholly unrelated sense (which would make theology impossible), but analogically — each name applying primarily to God and only secondarily, by derivation, to what He has caused.

> "Therefore it must be said that these names are said of God and creatures according to analogy, that is, according to proportion."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 13, A 5

### The First Namers of the Gods

Socrates' method in the *Cratylus*: treating the names of the gods as compressed philosophical claims, to be cracked open by etymology. His reading of "Zeus" as a fusion of "Zena" and "Dia" — one name, though divided — turns the god's very name into an argument, that he is the one "through whom all creatures always have life."

> "The name of Zeus... has also an excellent meaning, although hard to be understood, because really like a sentence, which is divided into two parts, for some call him Zena... and use the one half, and others who use the other half call him Dia... the two together signify the nature of the God... Wherefore we are right in calling him Zena and Dia, which are one name, although divided, meaning the God through whom all creatures always have life..."
> — Plato, *Cratylus*, 91c-d

### The Incommunicable Name

The Wisdom literature's diagnosis of the origin of idolatry: not primarily a philosophical error but a linguistic one, in which the unique name reserved for the true God is transferred, out of grief or flattery, onto a carved image. The passage treats the divine name itself as sacred property, capable of being misapplied to what cannot bear it.

> "And this was the occasion of deceiving human life: for men serving either their affection, or their kings, gave the incommunicable name to stones and wood."
> — Wisdom of Solomon, 14:20-21 (Apocrypha)

### The Name That Changes with the Tongue

In Dante's Paradise, Adam recalls that the name of God he used in Eden was not the name used afterward — "on earth called I; afterwards it was called El" — because human language itself is mortal and mutable, changing "as a leaf on a branch, which goes away and another comes." Even the name of the unchanging God turns out to be a casualty of changing human speech.

> "...the Supreme Good, whence comes the gladness that swathes me was on earth called I; afterwards it was called El; and that must needs me, for the custom of mortals is as a leaf on a branch, which goes away and another comes."
> — Dante, *Divine Comedy*, Paradise, XXVI [124-138]

### The Nameless God

Aeschylus's chorus addresses Zeus by a name it immediately confesses may not fit him at all, uncertain "whether he love that name or love it not." Voiced in the register of tragedy rather than theology, it is the same anxiety that will occupy Plato and Aquinas centuries later: that any single word offered to the divine might simply miss.

> "Zeus—whosoe'er He be, Whose state excels / All language syllables, / Knowing not so much / As whether He love that name or love it not;"
> — Aeschylus, *Agamemnon* [160-183]

## 6. Symbolism in psychological analysis

*[Full section text](6%20Symbolism%20in%20psychological%20analysis/6.%20Symbolism%20in%20psychological%20analysis.md)*

### The Ambiguous Word as Dream-Disguise

One specific mechanism of dream-distortion, distinct from picture-symbolism proper: because a single word can carry several unrelated meanings at once, the dream-work can condense two separate thoughts into one ambiguous word exactly as a joke does — so that some dream images need to be untangled as a pun rather than read as a fixed symbol at all.

> "A word, as the point of junction of a number of ideas, possesses, as it were, a predestined ambiguity, and the neuroses (obsessions, phobias) take advantage of the opportunities for condensation and disguise afforded by words quite as eagerly as do dreams."
> — Freud, *Interpretation of Dreams*, sec. D ("Regard for Representability")

### Auto-Symbolic Phenomena

Herbert Silberer's experimental demonstration, endorsed by Freud, that symbol-formation is not confined to sleep: forcing himself to think through an abstract problem while drowsy, Silberer found the thought itself would slip away and a concrete visual image would appear in its place, letting him catch the mind's translation from abstraction into picture in the very act of happening.

> "I remember that I have to correct a halting passage in an essay." / "Symbol. I see myself planing a piece of wood."
> — Silberer, quoted in Freud's *Interpretation of Dreams*, sec. D

### The Genetic Origin of Symbolism

Freud's deepest theoretical claim about why any given symbol means what it means: a symbol is not an arbitrary code but a fossil — the leftover trace of a time, early in the history of language and thought, when the symbol and the thing it now merely represents were not yet distinguished but were conceptually and linguistically one and the same.

> "What is today symbolically connected was probably united in primitive times, by conceptual and linguistic identity. The symbolic relationship seems to be a residue and reminder of a former identity."
> — Freud, *General Introduction to Psycho-Analysis*, Lecture 10 ("Symbolism in Dreams")

### The House as the Body

The single most consistent symbol in Freud's catalog, credited originally to the philosopher K. A. Scherner: the human body as a whole — not just the genitals — is regularly represented in dreams by a house, with architectural detail alone (a smooth façade against ledges and balconies one can grip) sufficient to encode the dreamer's own sex.

> "When the walls are quite smooth, the house means a man; when there are ledges and balconies which can be caught hold of, a woman."
> — Freud, *General Introduction to Psycho-Analysis*, Lecture 10

### Hysterical Symptoms as Memory-Symbols

The founding clinical discovery behind psychoanalysis, drawn from Breuer's treatment of Anna O.: a hysterical symptom is not arbitrary or meaningless but functions exactly like a public monument — a permanent trace of one specific, forgotten emotional event — which is why Freud can compare it directly to Charing Cross, a stone still marking a queen's funeral procession centuries after the mourners themselves are gone.

> "Our hysterical patients suffer from reminiscences. Their symptoms are the remnants and the memory symbols of certain (traumatic) experiences."
> — Freud, *Origin and Development of Psycho-Analysis*, First Lecture

### Repression

Freud's term (*Verdrängung*) for the hypothesized force that keeps a painful memory out of consciousness in the first place — inferred not directly but from the very resistance a patient shows when the analyst tries to bring that memory back, which Freud treats as indirect proof that something is being actively, not just passively, kept hidden.

> "I called this hypothetical process repression (Verdrängung) and considered that it was proved by the undeniable existence of resistance."
> — Freud, *Origin and Development of Psycho-Analysis*, Second Lecture

### The Sexual Origin of the Sense of Beauty

Freud extends symbolic and sublimation theory from dreams and neurosis into aesthetics itself: the capacity to feel beauty, though it presents itself as disinterested contemplation, is traced back to sexual attraction with its aim inhibited — pleasure in form and appearance standing in for a satisfaction no longer sought directly.

> "Its derivation from the realms of sexual sensation is all that seems certain; the love of beauty is a perfect example of a feeling with an inhibited aim."
> — Freud, *Civilization and Its Discontents*

### The Symbolic Relation

Freud's precise, narrow definition of "symbol" as a technical term, set apart from the other three ways a dream-element can stand for a hidden thought (substitution of part for whole, allusion, and imagery): a symbolic relation is constant and invariable, so that the same object translates to the same latent idea regardless of the individual dreamer's own associations.

> "We call a constant relation of this kind between a dream-element and its translation a symbolic one, and the dream-element itself a symbol of the unconscious dream-thought."
> — Freud, *General Introduction to Psycho-Analysis*, Lecture 10

### Typical Dream-Symbols: King and Queen

The best-known instance of Freud's fixed symbol-catalog: royalty in a dream stands, almost without exception, for the dreamer's own parents, with the dreamer correspondingly cast as prince or princess — a translation so constant that Freud treats it as needing no confirmation from the individual dreamer's own associations at all.

> "The Emperor and the Empress (King and Queen) in most cases really represent the dreamer's parents; the dreamer himself or herself is the prince or princess."
> — Freud, *Interpretation of Dreams*, sec. E ("Representation in Dreams by Symbols")

### Water as the Symbol of Birth

One of the rare typical symbols Freud grounds in biology rather than language: emerging from or plunging into water regularly stands for being born, a translation he traces to the literal double fact that every mammal begins life submerged in amniotic fluid, and that the mammalian line itself evolved from water-dwelling ancestors.

> "Birth is regularly expressed by some connection with water: we are plunging into or emerging from water, that is to say, we give birth or are being born."
> — Freud, *General Introduction to Psycho-Analysis*, Lecture 10

## 6a. The symbolism of dreams: their latent and manifest content

*[Full section text](6a%20The%20symbolism%20of%20dreams%20their%20latent%20and%20manifest%20content/6a.%20The%20symbolism%20of%20dreams%20their%20latent%20and%20manifest%20content.md)*

### Gates of Horn and Ivory

Penelope's own theory of dream-truth in the *Odyssey*: dreams are not uniformly reliable, but split by their point of origin into two kinds — those that pass through the gate of horn, which "mean something," and those through the gate of ivory, which are merely "fatuous." It is one of the oldest attempts in Western literature to distinguish a dream's surface imagery from whether that imagery can be trusted — an ancestor, at a great distance, of the manifest/latent distinction this whole section is built around.

> "There are two gates through which these unsubstantial fancies proceed; the one is of horn, and the other ivory. Those that come through the gate of ivory are fatuous, but those from the gate of horn mean something to those that see them."
> — Homer, *Odyssey*, Bk XIX

### Dream-Interpretation as Self-Authorization

In the *Choephoroe*, Orestes hears a secondhand report of his mother Clytemnestra's dream of suckling a serpent, and declares that "all coheres": the viper is himself, and the dream licenses the matricide he has already resolved to commit. The scene dramatizes something Freud will later insist on clinically — that an interpretation is never a neutral decoding of fixed symbols, but is shaped from the outset by the interpreter's own prior wish.

> "In my interpretation all coheres... She dies in her blood; and I am dragon-fanged to kill her as the dream would have me do."
> — Aeschylus, *Choephoroe* [523-552]

### The Dismissed Mother-Dream

Confronted with Oedipus's dread of the oracle, Jocasta waves it away with a commonplace: dreams of sleeping with one's mother are ordinary and meaningless, "nought" to the man who keeps a level head about them. Freud inverts this exact reassurance twenty-three centuries later, treating the very commonness of the "Oedipus dream" not as proof of its harmlessness but as evidence of a universal, heavily disguised wish (see *The Oedipus Dream*, below).

> "But fear not thou touching wedlock with thy mother. Many men ere now have so fared in dreams also: but he to whom these things are as nought bears his life most easily."
> — Sophocles, *Oedipus the King* [977-982]

### Artabanus's Naturalistic Theory of Dreams

Advising Xerxes not to trust a recurring dream as a message from a god, the counselor Artabanus proposes a wholly psychological account instead: a dream's content is simply drawn from whatever preoccupied the mind during the previous day. Twenty-four centuries before Freud's "day's residue," a Persian courtier in Herodotus already located the source of dreams in yesterday's waking thought rather than in prophecy.

> "The dreams that wander to and fro among mankind, I will tell thee of what nature they are... Whatever a man has been thinking of during the day is wont to hover round him in the visions of his dreams at night."
> — Herodotus, *History*, Bk VII

### The Skilled Interpreter of Resemblances

Aristotle's brief, strikingly modern account of dream-interpretation as a form of pattern recognition: dream-images are compared to reflections broken up by moving water, and the good interpreter is the one who can reconstruct the true shape from its scattered, distorted fragments. Freud cites this very passage approvingly in *The Interpretation of Dreams*, treating Aristotle as an unexpected ally against purely mechanical, cipher-based methods of interpretation.

> "The most skilful interpreter of dreams is he who has the faculty of observing resemblances... Skilful, indeed, would he be in interpreting such reflexions who could rapidly discern, and at a glance comprehend, the scattered and distorted fragments of such forms, so as to perceive that one of them represents a man, or a horse, or anything whatever."
> — Aristotle, *On Prophesying*, Ch 2

### The Humoral Theory of Dreams

Pertelote's answer to her husband Chanticleer's terrifying dream of a beast in the yard: dreams are not omens at all but symptoms, the by-product of digestive "repletions" and an excess of bodily humors, best treated with a laxative rather than interpreted. It is the section's clearest statement of a purely physiological, anti-prophetic theory of dreaming — voiced only so that Chanticleer can spend the rest of the tale rebutting it with exempla of dreams that turned out true.

> "Are you frightened by a vision? Dreams are, God knows, a matter for derision. Visions are generated by repletions and vapours and the body's bad secretions of humours overabundant in a wight."
> — Chaucer, *Nun's Priest's Tale*

### The Murdered Companion

Chanticleer's rebuttal to Pertelote: the exemplary tale of two traveling companions lodged apart for the night, one of whom appears three times in the other's dream, first pleading for help and finally displaying his wounds and naming the dung-cart where his murdered body has been hidden. Found true the next morning, it stands in the section as the paradigm case of a dream taken as direct, uncoded testimony rather than as a symptom requiring interpretation at all.

> "'Alas! for in an oxes stalle this night I shal be mordred ther I lye. Now help me, dere brother, er I dye'... 'Bihold my blody woundes, depe and wyde!... A carte ful of dong ther shaltow see, in which my body is hid ful prively.'"
> — Chaucer, *Nun's Priest's Tale*

### Dreams as Interpreters of Inclination

Montaigne's brief, almost offhand aside in the midst of a meditation on his own even temperament: his dreams are gentle because his waking desires are gentle, and dreams in general can be trusted to reveal a person's true inclinations, provided one has the skill to read them correctly. Three centuries before *The Interpretation of Dreams*, Montaigne already treats the dream as a faithful witness to unconscious desire rather than to the future.

> "I believe it to be true that dreams are faithful interpreters of our inclinations; but there is art required to sort and understand them."
> — Montaigne, *Essays*

### Dreams as Children of an Idle Brain

Mercutio's answer to Romeo's report of a prophetic dream: before Romeo can even say what it meant, Mercutio interrupts with the extravagant, mock-heroic Queen Mab speech and concludes that dreams are not messages at all but "the children of an idle brain," spun out of nothing by "vain fantasy." It is the play's most eloquent statement of the purely skeptical, deflationary view of dreaming that runs alongside the section's prophetic and psychoanalytic material.

> "True, I talk of dreams, which are the children of an idle brain, begot of nothing but vain fantasy, which is as thin of substance as the air."
> — Shakespeare, *Romeo and Juliet*, Act I, Sc IV

### The Sleepwalking Confession

Lady Macbeth, asleep and rubbing her hands as though washing them, speaks aloud the guilt she has suppressed and denied every waking hour — Duncan's murder surfacing unbidden in gesture and word while her conscious mind is switched off. Three centuries before Freud, Shakespeare stages almost exactly the psychoanalytic claim that what is repressed by day returns, undisguised, once the ordinary censor of waking consciousness is itself asleep.

> "Out, damned spot! out, I say!... Here's the smell of the blood still. All the perfumes of Arabia will not sweeten this little hand."
> — Shakespeare, *Macbeth*, Act V, Sc I

### Fancy and the Faculties of Sleep

Adam's explanation to a shaken Eve of where her disturbing dream came from: beneath Reason sits a lesser faculty, Fancy, which by day only reworks what the five senses supply, but which at night "wakes to imitate" Reason in its absence — and, working unsupervised, produces the "wilde work" and "misjoyning shapes" of dreams. It is a fully-formed pre-Freudian model of dream-formation: a lower, image-making faculty operating without the oversight of the higher, judging one.

> "Oft in her absence mimic Fansie wakes to imitate her; but misjoyning shapes, wilde work produces oft, and most in dreams, ill matching words and deeds long past or late."
> — Milton, *Paradise Lost*, Bk V

### The Untranslatable Dream-Truth

Falling asleep in a cart on the road to Moscow, Pierre dreams he is finally about to grasp some vast, unifying truth, which condenses at the moment of waking into the single word "harness." Startled awake by the groom before the thought completes itself, he feels "with horror" that the meaning has been destroyed in the crossing from sleep to waking. Tolstoy dramatizes, from the inside, exactly the gap this whole section is about: a manifest residue — one absurd word — left behind by a latent content that cannot survive translation into waking language.

> "'I want to understand what was revealing itself to me in my dream. One second more and I should have understood it all! But what am I to do? Harness, but how can I harness everything?' and Pierre felt with horror that the meaning of all he had seen and thought in the dream had been destroyed."
> — Tolstoy, *War and Peace*, Bk XI

### Wish-Fulfillment

Freud's central thesis, arrived at through the self-analysis of his own "dream of Irma's injection": that every dream, however painful or nonsensical its surface, represents a wish of the dreamer's as fulfilled. The manifest strangeness of dreams is not evidence against this theory — it is the very problem that the rest of the theory (condensation, displacement, censorship) exists to explain.

> "It is a perfectly valid psychic phenomenon, actually a wish-fulfillment; it may be enrolled in the continuity of the intelligible psychic activities of the waking state; it is built up by a highly complicated intellectual activity."
> — Freud, *The Interpretation of Dreams*

### The Dream-Censor

Freud's answer to why wish-fulfillment is so rarely obvious on a dream's surface: a second psychic agency stands between the wishing part of the mind and consciousness, admitting nothing that has not first been modified to suit its own standards — exactly as a political censor forces a writer into allusion and disguise rather than allowing outright suppression to show. This censoring agency, not any failure of memory, is what produces the distortion between what a dream latently means and what it manifestly shows.

> "The detailed correspondence between the phenomena of censorship and the phenomena of dream-distortion justifies us in presupposing similar conditions for both... one of which forms the wish expressed by the dream, while the other exercises a censorship over this dream-wish, thereby enforcing on it a distortion."
> — Freud, *The Interpretation of Dreams*

### Dream-Work

Freud's name for the entire process by which a latent dream-thought is converted into the strange, pictorial manifest dream actually remembered on waking — and, correspondingly, his name for interpretation itself as the reverse operation, working backward from the manifest surface to undo what the dream-work has done. Condensation and displacement, described below, are simply his account of how this one process operates.

> "The process by which the latent dream is transformed into the manifest dream is called the dream-work; while the reverse process, which seeks to progress from the manifest to the latent thoughts, is our work of interpretation; the work of interpretation therefore aims at demolishing the dream-work."
> — Freud, *A General Introduction to Psycho-Analysis*

### Condensation

The first operation of the dream-work: the manifest dream is always a compressed, abbreviated rendering of a much richer latent content, achieved by omitting elements outright, letting a single fragment stand for an entire complex of ideas, or fusing several latent elements — most vividly several different people — into one blurred composite figure in the manifest dream.

> "The first achievement of the dream-work is condensation; by this term we mean to convey the fact that the content of the manifest dream is less rich than that of the latent thoughts, is, as it were, a kind of abbreviated translation of the latter."
> — Freud, *A General Introduction to Psycho-Analysis*

### Displacement

The second operation of the dream-work, and in Freud's account entirely the work of the censorship: a psychically important latent element is replaced in the manifest dream by something merely adjacent to it — a remote allusion — while the emotional emphasis is shifted onto some unimportant element, so that the dream's manifest center of gravity has nothing to do with its latent one.

> "Displacement takes two forms; first, a latent element may be replaced, not by a part of itself, but by something more remote, something of the nature of an allusion; and, secondly, the accent may be transferred from an important element to another which is unimportant, so that the centre of the dream is shifted as it were, giving the dream a foreign appearance."
> — Freud, *A General Introduction to Psycho-Analysis*

### The Oedipus Dream

Freud's clinical generalization of the very dream Sophocles has Jocasta wave away as commonplace and harmless (see *The Dismissed Mother-Dream*, above): dreams of sexual intercourse with one's mother turn out, in his patients' analyses, to be far more frequent than their manifest content ever shows, because most of them are so thoroughly disguised by displacement and condensation that the dreamer denies ever having had one at all.

> "If, in conversation with my patients, I emphasize the frequency of the Oedipus dream—the dream of having sexual intercourse with one's mother—I elicit the answer: 'I cannot remember such a dream.'... I can assure the reader that disguised dreams of sexual intercourse with the dreamer's mother are far more frequent than undisguised dreams to the same effect."
> — Freud, *The Interpretation of Dreams*

## 6b. The symbolism of apparently normal acts: forgetting, verbal slips, errors

*[Full section text](6b%20The%20symbolism%20of%20apparently%20normal%20acts%20forgetting/6b%20.%20The%20symbolism%20of%20apparently%20normal%20acts%20forgetting.md)*

### Symptomatic and Accidental Acts

Freud's name for a category of behavior even more innocent-looking than a slip of the tongue: small, apparently purposeless fidgets — playing with clothing, a part of the body, some object at hand — that seem to serve no function at all, and precisely because they are so superfluous and unmotivated on the surface, turn out to be legible expressions of something going on underneath.

> "We call them accidental and symptomatic acts. They also appear to be unmotivated, insignificant and unimportant but, in addition to this, they have very clearly the feature of superfluity."
> — Freud, *General Introduction*

### Forgetting as a Revealed Wish

Freud's illustration of how "forgetting a resolution" can dramatize an unconscious motive, drawn from literature rather than the clinic: in Shaw's *Caesar and Cleopatra*, Caesar departs feeling that he meant to do something he can no longer recall — and only realizes at the last moment that the forgotten task was saying farewell to Cleopatra. The playwright uses ordinary forgetfulness as a device to show what Caesar could not consciously admit he wanted.

> "Caesar, when departing in the last scene, is pursued by the feeling that there was something else he intended to do which he had now forgotten. At last it turns out what it is to say farewell to Cleopatra."
> — Freud, *General Introduction*

### The Poet's Deliberate Slip

Freud's argument from artistic practice: when a writer has a character make a slip of the tongue on the page, the writer has composed that "accident" on purpose, word for word. The very fact that slips can be deliberately manufactured to reveal something about a character — distraction, fatigue, a hidden expectation — is itself evidence that ordinary, real-life slips are not mere noise but carry meaning too.

> "It often happens that a poet makes use of a slip of the tongue or some other error as a means of artistic expression. This fact in itself proves that he thinks the error, for instance, a slip of the tongue, has a meaning; for he constructs it intentionally... He wishes to reveal something by means of the slip..."
> — Freud, *General Introduction*

### The Frequency, Not Universality, of Meaningful Error

Freud's careful statement of exactly how strong his own claim is — deliberately weaker than it is sometimes taken to be. He does not assert that every slip or lapse has a hidden meaning, only that a meaning can be found often enough across the various forms of error that the phenomenon is worth taking seriously as a class, whatever the explanation of any single instance turns out to be.

> "We do not maintain—and for our purposes do not need to maintain—that every single mistake which occurs has a meaning, although I think that probable. It is enough for us to prove that such a meaning is relatively frequent in the various forms of errors."
> — Freud, *General Introduction*

## 6c. The symbolism of anxieties, obsessions, and other neurotic manifestations

*[Full section text](6c%20The%20symbolism%20of%20anxieties/6c.%20The%20symbolism%20of%20anxieties.md)*

### The Underworld as Symbol of Inner Torment

A strikingly proto-psychological reading of the classical underworld myths. Lucretius argues that Tantalus, Tityos, and Sisyphus are not literal torments waiting after death, but symbols for anxieties the living already inflict on themselves — groundless dread, gnawing desire, futile ambition — so that "the life of fools" is already its own hell, manufactured entirely by the guilty or fearful mind rather than imposed on it from outside.

> "But there is in life a dread of punishment for evil deeds, signal as the deeds are signal, and for atonement of guilt... the conscience stricken mind through boding fears applies to itself goads and frightens itself with whips... The life of fools at length becomes a hell here on earth."
> — Lucretius, *Nature of Things*, Bk III, [978-1023] 42d-43b

### The Furies as the Shape of Guilt

Orestes, driven mad after killing his mother, experiences his guilt not as an abstract feeling but as an externalized, hallucinated assault: monstrous goddesses with snaky hair and bloodshot eyes, visible only to him. His sister's flat reassurance that his eye "sees none of the things which thy fancy paints" underlines that the play is dramatizing psychological torment through concrete, symbolic figures rather than reporting an actual supernatural visitation.

> "Mother, I implore thee! let not loose on me those maidens with their bloodshot eyes and snaky hair. Ha! see, see where they approach to leap upon me!... Lie still, poor sufferer, on thy couch; thine eye sees none of the things which thy fancy paints so clear."
> — Euripides, *Orestes*, [255-259] 396c

### The Compulsive Symbolic Act

Lady Macbeth's sleepwalking scene dramatizes a symptom centuries before psychoanalysis had a theory for it: an unconscious, endlessly repeated physical act — rubbing her hands — that symbolically reenacts and tries to undo a guilt she cannot consciously face or confess, surfacing instead in sleep, gesture, and one fixed, obsessively returning phrase.

> "Out, damned spot! out, I say!... What, will these hands ne'er be clean?... Here's the smell of the blood still. All the perfumes of Arabia will not sweeten this little hand."
> — Shakespeare, *Macbeth*, Act V, Sc I, 306b-307a

### The Symbolic Substitution Behind an Obsessive Act

Freud's case of a widow performing a bizarre daily ritual: summoning her maid to view a stain she has just made, in a particular spot, on her tablecloth. Tracing the act back to her wedding night, Freud reads the table and its cover as substitutes for the marriage bed and its sheet — the same substitution dreams make constantly — so that a piece of ordinary dream-symbolism is here acted out compulsorily while awake, the disguised form in which a painful memory insists on repeating itself.

> "To keep up the similarity we must assume that she has substituted the table and table-cover for the bed and sheet. This might seem too arbitrary; but then we have not studied dream-symbolism in vain. In dreams a table is very often found to represent a bed. Bed and board together mean marriage, so that the one easily stands for the other."
> — Freud, *Repression*

### Anxiety as a Signal of Danger

Freud's revised, mature theory of anxiety, correcting his own earlier account: anxiety is not a leftover by-product of a blocked symptom, but a deliberate alarm the ego sounds in advance of a danger it has recognized. Symptoms, in turn, are not created merely to smother that anxiety — they are created to help the person avoid the very danger-situation whose approach the anxiety has already signalled.

> "Anxiety is a reaction to a situation of danger... It would be truer to say that symptoms are created so as to avoid a danger-situation whose approach has been signalled by the emergence of anxiety."
> — Freud, *Inhibitions, Symptoms, and Anxiety*
