# Glossary — 4a. The relation between univocal meaning and definition

Key terms and concepts discussed in this section, each with a representative quote drawn directly from the citations below.

## Agreement on the Name vs. the Definition

The Stranger's methodological warning at the start of the hunt for the Sophist: two people can share a word perfectly and still mean different things by it, because agreement about a name is not agreement about the thing. Real univocal communication requires working out a definition together — which is why the dialogue rehearses the method first on something easy (an angler) before risking it on something hard (a sophist).

> "We ought always to come to an understanding about the thing itself in terms of a definition, and not merely about the name minus the definition."
> — Plato, *Sophist*

## Univocal: Same Name, Same Definition

Aristotle's technical definition of the section's key term: two things are univocal with respect to a name not merely when the same word applies to both, but when the very definition that explains the word applies to both without change. Genus, species, and differentiae are predicated of their subjects univocally in exactly this sense — the definition transfers along with the name.

> "The word 'univocal' was applied to those things which had both name and definition in common."
> — Aristotle, *Categories*, CH 5

## A Definition Must Identify Its Own Subject

Among Aristotle's diagnostic tests for a bad definition: if the wording alone, detached from the term it supposedly defines, doesn't make clear what is being defined, the definition has failed at its basic job. He compares this to old paintings so stylized that without a caption naming the figures, no one could tell what — or whom — they depicted.

> "See if, when it is merely stated by itself, it is not evident what it defines: just as in the works of the old painters, unless there were an inscription, the figures used to be unrecognizable."
> — Aristotle, *Topics*, BK VI, CH 2

## Against Dichotomous Division

Aristotle's biological case against defining a species by a chain of successive either/or splits (Footed/Footless, Feathered/Featherless...): the method inevitably tears apart natural kinds that share several independent traits at once — ants fall on both sides of Feathered/Featherless, since some fly and some don't — because no single differentia, however qualified by its predecessors, can capture what a whole essence actually is.

> "We must define at the outset by a multiplicity of differentiae... No single differentia, I repeat, either by itself or with its antecedents, can possibly express the essence of a species."
> — Aristotle, *Parts of Animals*, BK I, CH 2–3

## The Notion Expressed by a Name Is Its Definition

Aquinas's compact formula, offered while explaining how any name at all can be applied to God: a name's meaning just is its definition — which is why even a name we can rightly apply to God without seeing His essence still fails to define Him the way "man" defines the essence of man, since we possess no such comprehensive account of the divine nature to serve as its definition.

> "For the notion expressed by the name is the definition."
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, Part I, Q 13, A 1

## Definitions as the Beginning of Reckoning

Hobbes locates the entire reliability of geometry — "the only science it hath pleased God hitherto to bestow on mankind" — in one discipline: settling what every term means before reasoning with it. Anyone who skips this step and reasons from loosely fixed words gets caught in their own language, struggling only to become more entangled, like a bird in birdlime.

> "A man that seeketh precise truth had need to remember what every name he uses stands for... or else he will find himself entangled in words, as a bird in lime twigs; the more he struggles, the more belimed. And therefore in geometry... men begin at settling the significations of their words; which settling of significations, they call definitions."
> — Hobbes, *Leviathan*, Part I

## Nominal vs. Real Definition

Pascal's crucial distinction: a purely nominal definition just assigns an arbitrary new name to something already clearly pointed out ("call every number divisible into two equal parts *even*"), and being arbitrary, it can never be contradicted. But the moment a word already has an accepted meaning and someone claims that meaning *is* some further thing — "time is the motion of a created thing" — that is no longer a definition at all but a disputable proposition wearing a definition's disguise.

> "It is not the nature of these things that I say is known by all; it is simply the relation between the name and the thing... For definitions are made only to point out the things named and not to reveal their nature."
> — Pascal, *Geometrical Demonstration*, Section 1

## A Definition Fixes Use, Not a Single Idea

Berkeley, arguing against the theory of abstract general ideas, points out that a rigorous definition doesn't actually pin a word to one determinate mental picture. "Triangle" is defined without specifying size, color, or the particular angles of the sides — so an enormous variety of distinct particular triangles all satisfy the same univocal definition equally well.

> "It is one thing for to keep a name constantly to the same definition, and another to make it stand everywhere for the same idea; the one is necessary, the other useless and impracticable."
> — Berkeley, *Human Knowledge*, Intro, Sect 18

## Three Sources of Definitional Obscurity

Madison's application of the section's problem to law: even the most carefully drafted statute remains equivocal until litigation and precedent gradually pin down its meaning, because obscurity in any definition can arise from three independent directions — the object itself may be indistinct, the mind conceiving it may be imperfect, or language, the medium carrying the thought across to another mind, may simply be inadequate to the task.

> "Here, then, are three sources of vague and incorrect definitions: indistinctness of the object, imperfection of the organ of conception, inadequateness of the vehicle of ideas."
> — *The Federalist*, Number 37

## Undefined Terms Cannot Rank Things

Darwin, wrestling with whether human races should count as separate species, points out that the whole question is unanswerable so long as "species" itself has no generally accepted definition — and that trying to settle it without one is exactly as hopeless as trying to decide, with no definitions of the terms at all, whether a given cluster of houses is a village, a town, or a city.

> "It is a hopeless endeavour to decide this point, until some definition of the term 'species' is generally accepted... We might as well attempt without any definition to decide whether a certain number of houses should be called a village, town, or city."
> — Darwin, *Descent of Man*
