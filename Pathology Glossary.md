# Pathology Glossary

Where the chapter talks about disease in its clinical register rather than its everyday one. Of 38 uses of "pathology"/"pathological" across the 32 sections, 36 are concentrated in the four psychoanalytic sections (6, 6a, 6b, 6c) — this is Freud's own working vocabulary, not a borrowed metaphor. The two outliers (1b, 1d) are just footnote citations to other books' titles and aren't included here. Six entries below, each a genuinely distinct idea rather than a repeat of [Disease Keywords](Disease%20Keywords.md) in different words.

## The Pathological Product

Freud's own classification places the ordinary dream on the same spectrum as illness: not a special, healthy exception to pathology, but its mildest and most universal member. Hysterical symptoms, obsessions, and delusions are the same family of thing, just further along.

> "You will have realized that the dream is a pathological product, the first member of the series which includes the hysterical symptom, the obsession, and the delusion among its members."
> — Freud, *General Introduction to Psycho-Analysis*

## Not Pathological, Just Normal

Freud is careful to draw the line the other way too: not every strong or lingering emotion after loss counts as pathology. Grief that looks intense is still, on its own, ordinary — the diagnostic line sits somewhere else entirely.

> "A fixation on thoughts of the dead so short a time after death is certainly not pathological, but rather corresponds to normal emotional behavior."
> — Freud, *Origin and Development of Psycho-Analysis*

## The Method Discovered on Symptoms First

Freud's own account of how he arrived at dream-interpretation is not what the chapter's dream material might suggest: he did not start with dreams and extend the method to illness. He started with illness, resolving "psycho-pathological structures" for therapeutic reasons for years, and only afterward realized the same method applied to dreams.

> "For years I have been occupied with the resolution of certain psycho-pathological structures — hysterical phobias, obsessional ideas, and the like — with therapeutic intentions."
> — Freud, *Interpretation of Dreams*

## Resistance Maintains the Pathology

Pathology, on this account, is not simply present or absent — it is actively held in place by a force the patient is themselves exerting, felt clinically as resistance to treatment. The pathological state is a thing being maintained, not just a thing that happened.

> "One could get an idea of this force, which maintained the pathological situation, from the resistance of the patient."
> — Freud, *Origin and Development of Psycho-Analysis*

## The Weakened Censor

Freud's mechanistic account of how a psychic state turns pathological rather than merely dreamlike: either the ordinary censorship between unconscious and conscious mind is itself pathologically weakened, or the unconscious excitations pressing against it are pathologically strengthened. Either failure, on its own, is enough.

> "Not by the decline at night in the energy put forth by the critical censorship, but by the pathological enfeeblement of the latter, or the pathological reinforcement of the unconscious excitations."
> — Freud, *Interpretation of Dreams*

## Ordinary Medicine Deserts the Physician

A physician's real training — anatomy, physiology, pathology — is presented as no help at all in front of hysteria. It isn't that the doctor lacks knowledge; it's that hysteria doesn't answer to the kind of knowledge pathology trains a physician to have.

> "All his knowledge, his anatomical-physiological and pathological education, desert him. He cannot understand hysteria."
> — Freud, *Origin and Development of Psycho-Analysis*
